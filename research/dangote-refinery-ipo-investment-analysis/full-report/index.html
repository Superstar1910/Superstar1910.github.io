Original underwriting conclusion — 17 September 2026

Historical view: this section preserves the original prospectus-based underwrite. The 18 October 2026 update above supersedes its valuation range, entry threshold and action framework.

DPRP is becoming a world-scale operating refinery; that does not make the IPO an attractive security at any price. At ₦525, investors pay approximately US$47.8bn for the equity and about US$47.7bn enterprise value after assumed deployment of net IPO cash. The price is equivalent to 9.2× mechanically annualised H1 2026 EBITDA and 13.1× annualised H1 earnings. US refiners trade around a 9.4× median LTM EV/EBITDA, but they offer longer operating histories, deeper liquidity, diversified assets, mature governance and lower-cost funding. DPRP therefore warrants a discount—not parity—on normalised earnings.

Action
PASS / WAIT FOR PROOF

Do not subscribe at ₦525. Revisit below ₦370 only after a facility-by-facility funding plan, sustained through-cycle margins and a fully diluted capitalisation are disclosed. A disciplined entry threshold is approximately ₦280, allowing a margin of safety to the constructive existing-operations value.

Investment thesis and variant perception

The asset is increasingly proven

US EIA data show 700 kb/d capacity after February maintenance; Nigeria shipped 561 kb/d of petroleum products in 2Q26, including 350 kb/d exports. Reuters/Kpler evidence indicates 565 kb/d Nigerian crude receipts in August and at least 520 kb/d October purchases. This materially reduces the “can it operate?” risk.

The earnings base is not yet proven

Gross margin moved from –9.5% in 2024 to 1.9% in 2025 and 17.9% in H1 2026. H1 contains only four months after management says stable full-capacity operations began. Product prices rose sharply and 2026 refining conditions benefited from global supply disruption.

The expansion is an option, not a completed asset

IPO cash funds just 10.85% of a US$14.269bn plan. Completion is variously stated as 2029 and 2030. Without committed funding, EPC protections, a crude-supply plan and project returns, assigning full multiple value to the second train transfers development risk to public investors.

Minority protections do not offset controller risk

The offer creates only 3.3% public ownership; beneficial control remains c.84.4%. Audit and Risk is chaired by a DIL executive, committees were newly constituted and untested, and several key policies were still being adopted.

Variant wedge: consensus enthusiasm is anchored on scarcity, scale and annualised H1 earnings. Our variant view is that the share price must be underwritten on post-working-capital, post-maintenance and post-expansion-financing returns. On that basis the current offer pays today for much of tomorrow’s execution.

Balance sheet and cash-quality points

  • At 30 June, debt was US$5.666bn, cash US$4.267bn and net debt US$1.398bn. The apparent improvement was capital-structure driven: US$4.0bn “project coreshift” proceeds and US$2.477bn share deposits accompanied repayment of the US$3.987bn DIL loan.
  • Receivables rose from ₦1.679tn to ₦4.029tn in six months; inventory rose 46% to ₦2.816tn. Post-period collection evidence is essential.
  • H1 reported capex was only US$37.9m, while the disclosed remaining-2026 plan is US$4.8bn. H1 “free cash flow” is therefore not distributable cash flow.
  • H1 earnings include a net FX gain and benefited from very large realised-price increases. Through-cycle gross refining margin, not annualised PAT, should anchor valuation.
  • Positive pre-operation CFO in 2021–23 came from contractor payables building despite zero revenue; it is not recurring cash-generation evidence.

DPRP’s moat is actual availability, scale, complexity, deep-water logistics and integrated evacuation—not simply nameplate capacity. Nigeria’s four state refineries have 445 kb/d combined nameplate capacity but have historically been offline or intermittent. Yet DPRP’s output is already too large for a purely domestic narrative: export markets are essential and expose shareholders to global cracks, freight and destination-market rules.

Cycle warning: the IEA projects global refined-product demand peaking in 2027 at 86.3m b/d. It expects 4.2m b/d of new capacity through 2030 against 1.6m b/d of closures—net supply growth above demand growth. 2026 disruption margins should not be terminalised.

Carbon transition and export-market exposure

DPRP may be physically competitive, but it cannot yet be placed reliably on a global carbon-cost curve. Its new asset base, Nelson Complexity Index of 11.5, broad crude flexibility, Euro-V product capability and 570MW captive plant support conversion and product-quality competitiveness. Conversely, coking, hydrocracking, RFCC, hydrotreating and hydrogen production are energy intensive. Complexity is not evidence of low emissions.

DCF / SOTP and WACC cross-check

A US-dollar DCF is appropriate because feedstock, product prices, debt and capex are predominantly dollar-linked. A reasonable operating-asset WACC is 14–16%: c.5% US risk-free rate, mature-market equity premium and refining beta, Nigeria country/equity risk and a company/governance premium, partly offset by debt. The 18–20% range remains appropriate for unfunded expansion cash flows. Approximately 12% is required for the existing cash-flow shape to approach ₦525—too low before operating, financing and governance risks are resolved.
