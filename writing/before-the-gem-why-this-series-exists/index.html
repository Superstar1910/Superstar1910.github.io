Finding Venture Capital Returns in Public Markets

Originally published on Substack: 2026-08-22 · Original publication ↗

In development finance, there is a term that does more work than almost any other in the field. The term is additionality.

Additionality asks a precise and uncomfortable question. Not: is this intervention good? Not: does this project create value? But: would this value have existed anyway, without this specific intervention, at this specific moment? If the answer is yes — if the capital would have flowed, the project would have been built, the outcome would have arrived regardless — then the intervention has no additionality. It has participated in something. It has not added to it.

The concept is demanding because it forces honesty about counterfactuals. It insists that value creation and value addition are different things. You can do something real and useful and still fail the additionality test — if what you did would have been done anyway, by someone else, with someone else’s capital, on someone else’s timeline.

I have spent most of my professional life working in markets where additionality is not a rhetorical device. It is a condition of investment. DFIs ask for it. Blended finance structures are built around it. Capital that cannot demonstrate additionality does not get the concessions, the guarantees, or the co-investors.

It is the right lens through which to introduce this series.

What Was Already There

There is no shortage of investment content. Substack alone hosts thousands of publications that cover public markets, equity analysis, special situations, and emerging market investing. The best of them are genuinely excellent — precise, honest, and built on real analytical work.

There is also no shortage of content about Africa’s economic transformation. The rise of mobile money. The demographic dividend. The infrastructure gap. The venture capital flowing into Lagos and Nairobi and Accra. This content exists. It is often well-written and, on its own terms, accurate.

What did not exist — or did not exist in the form this series attempts — was the intersection of those two bodies of thinking, applied through a specific, portable, replicable analytical framework.

The series you are about to read is called Embedded Call Options. Its central argument is that public markets frequently misprice companies where a mature, visible, already-priced business hides a fast-growing, platform-like, dramatically undervalued asset — and that this mispricing is most extreme, most systematic, and most exploitable in African markets right now.

That argument is not new in its components. Hidden asset investing has a long tradition. Conglomerate discounts are well-documented in academic finance. The African fintech revolution has been covered extensively. None of those things required this series.

What required this series — what justifies its existence under the additionality test — is the framework that connects them.

The Additionality Case

First: the vocabulary did not exist.

Investment frameworks only compound if they are named. Howard Marks did not invent the idea of market cycles. But he named them, mapped them, and made the language precise enough that investors could apply the thinking without him in the room. Aswath Damodaran did not invent discounted cash flow. But his systematisation of valuation made it teachable, transferable, and honest about its own limitations.

The Embedded Call Option framework — the Rump, the Gem, the SOTP Gap, the distinction between Free Optionality and a Value Trap — is an attempt to do the same thing for a specific type of mispricing that has no widely shared name. Until investors have the vocabulary, they cannot reliably see the thing the vocabulary describes. The vocabulary is not decoration. It is the enabling condition for the analysis.

Second: the Africa-specific case was not being made in public markets.

The conversation about African fintech is almost entirely a venture capital conversation. It concerns itself with valuations of private companies, the emergence of unicorns, the growth of Series B and C rounds in Lagos and Nairobi. This is a real and important conversation. But it is taking place at the wrong end of the market.

Africa’s largest, most scaled, most consequential fintech infrastructure is not in those venture portfolios. It is inside MTN, inside Safaricom, inside Airtel Africa, inside Car and General — listed on African exchanges, priced at the multiples appropriate to their legacy businesses, and almost entirely absent from the fintech conversation that dominates coverage of the continent’s digital economy.

The investor who understands this is looking at a different set of assets than the investor who does not. This series makes that case with the analytical rigour it requires — not as a thematic observation, but as a specific, valuation-grounded, actionable framework.

Third: the risk management layer was missing.

Hidden asset investing has a literature. But much of it focuses on the opportunity without equal attention to the trap. The persistent holdco discount. The VIE structure that creates the illusion of ownership without the substance. The family-controlled African conglomerate where the Gem is real, the SOTP gap is measurable, and the minority investor has no mechanism to capture it.

A framework without its failure modes is not a framework. It is a sales pitch. Article 4 of this series — The Illusion of Ownership — exists because the discipline of knowing when not to apply the framework is as important as the framework itself. That article, in this series, is the additionality that separates an intellectual exercise from an investing practice.

Who This Series Is For

It is for the investor who covers African equities and has not yet built the analytical vocabulary to surface the fintech value embedded in the listed companies they already follow.

It is for the global investor who understands conglomerate discounts and hidden asset investing but has not applied that lens to African markets, where the pricing anomalies are more extreme than anywhere else in the world.

It is for the reader who has followed Africa’s fintech story through a venture capital lens and has not yet looked at what is sitting, mispriced and underappreciated, inside the continent’s listed equity markets.

And it is for anyone who has ever bought a company and later discovered that the part they had not modelled was the part that mattered most.

What Comes Next

The series runs five articles over five weeks.

Part 1: The Gem Inside the Rump introduces the framework through Car and General and MTN Uganda. If you read only one article, read this one.

Part 2: Africa’s Hidden Fintechs scales the argument to the continent MTN MoMo, M-Pesa, Airtel Money, Watu Credit and makes the systematic case for why African markets produce embedded optionality discounts more reliably than anywhere else.

Part 3: The Greatest Hidden Asset Trades in the World proves the framework is not Africa-specific. The same mispricing, at global scale, inside the most-followed companies on earth.

Part 4: The Illusion of Ownership is the discipline layer. Not every hidden asset unlocks. Some are structurally trapped. This article teaches you the difference.

Part 5: How to Invest in Embedded Optionality converts the framework into portfolio practice — classification, sizing, expected return arithmetic, recession behavior, and a full African market watchlist.

The additionality of this series is not in the individual observations. It is in the framework that makes those observations replicable, a lens you will carry out of this series and apply, independently, to every listed company you look at from here forward.

That is what it is trying to add.
