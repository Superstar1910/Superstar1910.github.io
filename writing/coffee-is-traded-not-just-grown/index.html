RESEARCH / COFFEE PRICING ENGINE

Kenneth S. Legesi, CFA · 29 October 2026 · Research essay

Coffee Is Traded, Not Just Grown

The challenge for coffee-producing economies is not simply to produce more coffee. It is to understand — and capture — more of the economics of the coffee they produce.

A farmer sees a price per kilogram. A trader sees an inventory position. An exporter sees a grade, a shipment, a payment date and a buyer obligation. A large international trading house sees all of these at once, alongside futures prices, origin differentials, currency, credit exposure and the cost of carrying stock.

They may be trading the same coffee, but they are not operating with the same information.

This is one of the reasons I have begun work on the Coffee Pricing Engine: a public-facing tool that seeks to connect the physical economics of coffee production with the financial economics of coffee trading.

A market price is not a business model

Suppose a coffee producer hears that Robusta is selling for UGX 24,000 per kilogram in Kampala. Is that a good price? It is impossible to answer without specifying the coffee form, grade, moisture, buyer, date and delivery terms — and knowing how much it cost the seller to produce or acquire that kilogram.

For a farmer, the right denominator begins with productive trees, the yield of a plot, inputs, labour, losses and actual saleable output. For an aggregator, the farmer's selling price becomes an acquisition cost. For a processor, the question becomes how much acceptable green coffee emerges from each kilogram of cherry, dried cherry or parchment. For an exporter, the costs extend into quality assurance, storage, finance, freight, compliance and payment risk.

In each case, the headline price is only one variable.

The invisible economics between tree and trade

A shipment's apparent spread can disappear through poor milling outturn, slow inventory turnover, a stronger purchasing currency, delayed buyer settlement or a quality claim.

Conversely, an enterprise can create value without taking an outsized price view: through better farm yield, more consistent quality, lower losses, faster turnover, cheaper working capital or more disciplined contracting.

The goal is not to predict every movement in London Robusta futures. It is to make the enterprise's own cost and risk structure legible.

An open methodology, not another mysterious price signal

The Coffee Pricing Engine begins with a simple question: what does one saleable kilogram really cost? The answer must be reconcilable from farm records, processing outturns and documented transaction expenses. It must then be tested against the price the market will actually pay for the same product, quality, place and date.

A cost-based target is not a market-clearing price. Where the achievable market netback falls below cost, the engine should show the economic gap rather than conjure a margin.

Our current calculator is an illustrative prototype, not a live market quotation service. The research programme is developing validated conversion tables, costing principles, finance schedules and buyer-price comparisons before stronger claims can be made.

Why this matters

Improved access to market prices helps. But it is insufficient if the producer cannot calculate cost per tree, the huller cannot reconcile saleable outturn, or the trader cannot quantify what 45 days of borrowed capital does to a deal.

The ambition is to move from prices heard and repeated to costs measured and decisions explained.

Know your cost before you quote.

Contribute to the research

We welcome anonymised farm-year cost and yield data, real milling outturn sheets, grade-recovery measurements, dated physical coffee bids and exporter cost schedules. Particularly valuable are observations that challenge the model. Share the type of data, coffee form, units, observation date, region and permission to use it through the site's Connect route. Do not send confidential personal, commercial or customer information without permission or an agreed data-sharing arrangement.
