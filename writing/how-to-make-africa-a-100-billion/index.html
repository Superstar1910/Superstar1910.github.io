How to Make Africa a $100 Billion Tradeable Asset Class

Originally published on Substack: 2026-03-18 · Original publication ↗

For decades, the conversation around investing in Africa has been pulled between two poles: the moral weight of “aid” and the high-octane promise of “frontier” private equity. But as global capital gathered in London for the AFBC Africa Investment Summit 2026, a quieter, more structural theme cut through the noise.

Liquidity.

At a panel discussion, Herta von Stiegel, Founder & CEO of Ariya Capital Group, delivered a statement that should be pinned to every investment committee wall allocating to Africa:

“Donor capital is $1 trillion. If we take 10% of that into commercial capital, it will move the needle on the continent. We need a secondary market to allow this capital to be invested.”

That is the challenge in one sentence. Africa does not have a capital shortage. It has a capital circulation problem. Every investment is treated as a unique, decade-long commitment — bespoke, illiquid, and trapped. Until we change that, until we start treating African assets as components of a liquid, tradeable asset class, we are running a high-powered engine with the handbrake on.

The End of “Easy Aid”

The era of predictable, large-scale concessional lending from a handful of Western powers is over. We have entered a multipolar world where Washington, Beijing, Brussels, and Riyadh all compete for influence — but none of them are writing blank cheques anymore.

The numbers are stark. In 2024, global Official Development Assistance fell by 7.1% in real terms — the sharpest single-year decline in over half a decade. The UK and US have signalled further cuts of up to 17% through 2026. The $1 trillion donor pool is no longer a growing endowment. It is a finite, shrinking resource that must work harder.

At the same time, Africa’s domestic institutional capital — pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds — has crossed the $1.1 trillion milestone. We now have two trillion-dollar pools sitting on opposite sides of a broken bridge. The missing structure is not more money. It is the secondary market that allows capital to flow between them.

The implication is clear: donor capital must stop being a grant and start being a catalyst. Deploy it to build and de-risk assets, then exit — recycling that capital into the next frontier of risk, the next early-stage project, the next underserved market. One dollar doing the work of ten.

The Sustainability Crisis in Philanthropy

The same logic is reshaping how foundations and philanthropists operate. For years, “impact” was measured in “lives touched.” That metric is no longer sufficient — or financially sustainable.

The SDG funding gap now stands at $4 trillion annually and is widening in a high-inflation, high-interest-rate environment. The old model — grant $1, watch it disappear, appeal for another $1 — is structurally bankrupt.

Forward-thinking foundations are shifting toward what can be called the “1.05 Model”: deploy $1 into a high-impact project, absorb the early-stage risk, extract the social value, and eventually recover the principal plus a modest return. That $1.05 is then redeployed into the next frontier project. A static endowment becomes a perpetual impact engine.

But without a secondary market, this engine stalls. Donor capital becomes stuck in mature, de-risked assets — a solar park that has been operational for five years, generating stable cash flows — because there is no standardised venue to sell that stake to a commercial investor. Those billions cannot be recycled. The next wave of green hydrogen projects, early-stage carbon capture ventures, and frontier infrastructure sits unfunded while perfectly good capital collects dust in brownfield assets.

How the Iran War Reshaped the Perception of Risk

The urgency of building a tradeable African asset class has been dramatically accelerated by an event most Western investors never put in their Africa models: the Iran War.

This conflict has done something remarkable. It has inverted the traditional risk hierarchy.

Previously, “high risk” in an African context meant local political instability or currency volatility. In the post-Iran War landscape, the dominant definition of risk has shifted to dependency — specifically, dependency on supply chains that must navigate the Strait of Hormuz or the Suez Canal. A 40% redirection of long-haul trade toward South Atlantic and African coastal corridors is already underway, according to Allianz Trade’s Global Trade Outlook 2026.

Paradoxically, this makes many African markets look more resilient than their Middle Eastern or Eastern European “Emerging Market” peers. An infrastructure project in East Africa — powered by geothermal energy, serving regional trade — is increasingly viewed as decorrelated from the primary volatility of a Eurasian conflict theater. For the first time in a decade, African real assets are showing a correlation coefficient below 0.3 with Eurasian volatility indices.

With Sub-Saharan Africa projected to grow at 4.3% in 2026 against a global average of 2.7%, the continent is no longer just a “high-risk bet.” It is becoming a geographic diversifier — a safety valve against Eurasian instability that sophisticated allocators are only beginning to price correctly.

Mobilising Africa’s Own $1.1 Trillion

So who buys the de-risked assets when donor capital exits? The answer has been sitting in plain sight.

African pension funds and insurance companies — the continent’s own institutional investors — are the natural long-term buyers. They are inherently long on Africa’s future. They need stable, inflation-hedged, long-duration yields. A de-risked infrastructure asset is almost purpose-built for their mandates.

And yet the numbers tell a story of stunning underallocation:

  • African institutional assets under management: $1.1 trillion

  • Average allocation to infrastructure and real assets: less than 5%

  • Average allocation to private equity: below 1% in most markets

  • In Kenya, where regulations permit up to 10% in private equity, actual deployment stands at just 0.08%

This is not a question of appetite. It is a question of access and fiduciary constraint. African pension funds hold 60–75% of their portfolios in government bonds — not because they prefer it, but because government bonds are liquid, regulated, and tradeable. Private infrastructure assets are not. There is no standardised, transparent venue to buy or sell them. There is no easy exit. And without an exit, no fiduciary can justify the entry.

A functioning secondary market is the institutional handshake that closes this gap. It allows the donor to absorb the high-risk construction phase, then pass a stabilised, cash-flowing asset to the pension fund at the operational phase. Both parties get what they need. Capital circulates. The continent benefits.

Shifting just 5% of African institutional AUM into real assets would unlock $55 billion in new investment. Shift 10%, and you reach the $100 billion target von Stiegel identified. The capital is already there. It is waiting for a market to flow into.

The Mandate: Three Structural Shifts

Moving from bespoke projects to a liquid market is not a question of intent — it is a systems engineering problem. Three structural changes are non-negotiable.

1. Standardisation of Assets

Investors cannot trade what they cannot compare. A bond in Kenya must behave like a bond in Nigeria — same ESG reporting standards, same credit enhancement structures, same legal documentation. Without a common financial syntax, due diligence takes months and transaction costs alone eliminate viable returns. Standardisation compresses that to days and makes cross-border comparison possible at scale.

2. Blended Finance as Credit Enhancement

Donor capital must evolve from funding projects to providing “first-loss” cushions. When a donor or DFI absorbs the initial 10% of downside risk, the remaining 90% of a project is effectively elevated to investment-grade status — legally eligible for conservative pension fund mandates. This single structural shift can unlock billions currently sitting on the sidelines, waiting for a risk profile that meets fiduciary standards. It is not charity. It is intelligent capital deployment.

3. Digital Exchange Platforms

The current over-the-counter deal model is opaque, slow, and inaccessible to most institutional buyers. Transparent digital listing platforms with fractionalised ownership of infrastructure debt create the visible, accessible exit mechanism that liquidity requires. This is not a distant ambition. It is an engineering problem with existing solutions, waiting to be applied at continental scale.

What Is Being Done — and What Still Needs to Happen

Progress is real, and it matters. Strategic pilots are already proving the model works:

British International Investment (BII) recently executed a landmark secondary sale of a private equity portfolio to private investors, demonstrating that development finance institutions can and do exit — and recycle capital in the process.

FSD Africa is actively funding thematic green and infrastructure bonds listed on local exchanges, including the Nairobi Securities Exchange, creating visible and accessible trading venues where none existed before.

The African Exchange Linkage Project (AELP) is connecting seven major stock exchanges, enabling seamless cross-border asset trading between London, Nairobi, Casablanca, and Lagos — laying the circulatory infrastructure the market needs.

African institutional consortiums are moving pension funds from passive observers to active co-investors alongside DFIs, closing the distance between development finance and commercial scale.

But three critical gaps remain. Until they are closed, the needle will not move at the scale the moment demands.

Harmonised Securitisation Frameworks. Most African markets lack the legal infrastructure to bundle smaller projects into large, tradeable Asset-Backed Securities. Without this, the market remains permanently stuck in “small deal” territory — unable to reach the size that attracts global institutional capital or justifies the fixed costs of market participation.

Currency Hedging at Scale. Local currency volatility currently adds a 400–600 basis point premium to African transactions — a hidden tax that silently erodes commercial returns and keeps foreign capital at arm’s length. Project-level hedges are not enough. What is needed is a systemic, DFI-backed currency fund that pools risk across a basket of African currencies. By diversifying exposure at scale and deploying a first-loss capital layer against extreme tail risks, such a facility — a “Super-TCX” model — could reduce the risk premium on African infrastructure by 200–300 basis points, transforming illiquid local assets into globally investable products.

Regulatory Passporting. A fund manager in Ghana should be able to acquire a de-risked asset in Tanzania without navigating an entirely new regulatory framework from scratch. Pan-African regulatory passporting, aligned with the AELP and the AfCFTA Protocol on Investment, would allow capital to flow across linked exchanges without friction. It is the difference between a network and a collection of isolated nodes.

The Bottom Line

Africa does not need more aid. It needs better plumbing.

The $1 trillion in donor capital, the $1.1 trillion in domestic institutional assets, and the growing recognition of Africa as a geopolitical safe haven have created a rare and time-sensitive alignment of conditions. The raw material for a $100 billion tradeable asset class already exists. What is missing is the market infrastructure to bring it to life — the standardisation, the credit enhancement, the digital platforms, the regulatory bridges.

The secondary market is not a financial abstraction. It is the mechanism by which one dollar does the work of ten — de-risking an asset, proving its viability, attracting institutional capital, and cycling back to do it all again at the next frontier.

Africa’s investment story is no longer about potential. It is about plumbing.

And the blueprint is already on the table.
