Kenneth S. Legesi, CFA · Research and reflection · Luck, Risk & Wealth

Luck Favours the Prepared. But Not Always.

We like stories in which discipline produces success and mistakes explain failure. Real life is less orderly.

The question behind the outcome

Across this series we have explored five forces: effort, luck, risk, ownership and time. Effort develops skills and creates options. Luck changes which doors appear and whether a sensible choice pays off. Risk determines what we can survive. Ownership gives us a claim on the value created. Time allows learning and compounding.

What makes this difficult

No single force is sufficient. Preparation cannot eliminate misfortune. Good timing cannot repair a permanently weak business model. Aggressive risk-taking can generate impressive short-term gains while quietly raising the chance of ruin. And the privileges that make patience possible are not equally distributed.

A more useful way forward

The practical response is neither fatalism nor self-congratulation. Build capabilities, seek productive relationships, acquire assets thoughtfully, preserve liquidity and judge decisions by process as well as outcome. Where we can, help build institutions that widen access to opportunity for others.

The uncomfortable limits of the success story

We are drawn to explanations that make outcomes feel orderly. The successful founder worked relentlessly. The investor spotted an overlooked trend. The professional built an exceptional network. These explanations can be partly true while remaining incomplete. They often omit favourable timing, supportive institutions and the many people who made similar efforts without receiving similar rewards.

Luck does not invalidate achievement. It changes how achievement should be interpreted. If we credit ourselves with every favourable event, we may become overconfident and take risks that our skill cannot justify. If we blame ourselves for every setback, we may abandon sound methods or overlook structural obstacles.

Five forces acting together

Effort is the part we most readily recognise. It includes learning, showing up, improving execution and persisting when progress is slow. Luck includes starting conditions, chance encounters and outcomes that remain uncertain after a decision. Risk determines what can be lost and whether a person can recover.

Ownership changes how effort participates in future value. A worker can earn a good income without acquiring an asset; an owner may receive returns long after the initial work, but also bears exposure to loss. Time allows both advantages and mistakes to compound. A sensible strategy given enough time can create options; a weak strategy pursued stubbornly can magnify damage.

None of these forces works independently. An early opportunity may be valuable only because someone developed the skill to recognise it. Ownership may be possible only because previous income created savings. Time may help only when the person has enough liquidity to avoid a forced sale.

What preparation can genuinely achieve

Preparation cannot guarantee a favourable outcome. It can improve the quality of choices available when an opportunity arrives. Strong financial records can help a business negotiate financing. A trusted professional reputation can open a conversation. Technical knowledge can reveal a risk that an enthusiastic crowd ignores.

Preparation also includes restraint. The investor who declines a transaction because repayment rights are unclear may miss a profitable opportunity, but avoids an exposure that was never properly understood. Judgement is measured over many decisions, not by the most exciting anecdote.

From personal wealth to better systems

There is a broader implication. If wealth depends partly on institutions and access, financial education alone is not enough. Reliable property rights, transparent capital markets, effective consumer protection and credible financing vehicles help convert effort into durable assets. Better systems do not eliminate luck, but they can reduce the extent to which arbitrary barriers determine who gets a chance.

We can contribute at several levels: manage our own risks, build businesses with sound governance, share useful knowledge and support mechanisms that broaden participation in ownership. This is a more demanding ambition than simply celebrating individual winners.

The lesson of this series is not to stop working hard or to wait for fortune. It is to combine preparation with humility, ownership with risk management and ambition with patience. Improve the odds where you can. Protect the capacity to continue when you are wrong. And remember that a life cannot be judged fairly from its financial outcome alone.

Field note: Financial success is not proof of perfect judgement. The more useful ambition is to improve the odds, preserve dignity through uncertainty and make room for others to participate.

What would you change in your own financial decisions after examining this idea? The point of this series is not to offer a shortcut to wealth, but to make the trade-offs clearer and the decisions more deliberate.
