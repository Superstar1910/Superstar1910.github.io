Luck, Risk & Wealth · Essay 01 of 12

Kenneth S. Legesi, CFA · Research and reflection · Luck, Risk & Wealth

Same Effort. Different Outcomes.

Two people can work equally hard and arrive at very different financial destinations.

The question behind the outcome

We often evaluate success as though everyone began from the same starting line. But starting capital, family obligations, networks, geography and the timing of opportunity differ enormously. Effort matters; the conversion of effort into ownership matters too.

What makes this difficult

The useful question is not whether successful people worked hard. Many did. It is whether their effort operated alongside favourable conditions, access to assets and tolerable risk. A salary can reward work without necessarily creating a claim on future growth.

A more useful way forward

Map your starting conditions without shame or self-congratulation. Identify which advantages you can cultivate: skills, trusted relationships, savings capacity and access to ownership. Recognise the constraints that cannot be wished away.

The same work, different starting lines

Imagine two professionals who are equally diligent. Both study, accept demanding assignments and save what they can. One can live with family while building an emergency fund. The other sends a substantial share of each pay cheque home. One has a relative who can introduce potential investors; the other must establish every relationship from scratch. Neither person's effort is fictional. Yet their capacity to absorb a failed venture, take a career break or purchase an asset is not the same.

These differences accumulate. An early asset purchase can appreciate and become collateral for another investment. A period of unemployment can force a second person to sell investments before they mature. The compounding effect applies to advantages and disadvantages alike. The point is not that individual choices are irrelevant, but that choices are made within unequal sets of options.

Why outcomes can mislead us

Success stories usually begin at the moment a decision becomes visible: the business launch, the investment, the promotion. The years of support, relationships and failed experiments may remain hidden. We also hear more from the winners than from people who followed comparable strategies and did not succeed. This selection effect makes a risky path appear more reliable than it was.

When evaluating a successful entrepreneur, ask what had to go right and what could have gone wrong. Was there a strong market? Did the founder have access to patient capital? Could the household withstand several years without income? These are not attempts to discount achievement. They are attempts to understand its conditions and decide what, if anything, can be replicated.

Turning perspective into a plan

Begin with a household balance sheet: liquid savings, debts, investments, earning capacity and obligations. Then distinguish between the assets you own and the income you currently earn. A promotion improves cash flow, but unless part of the surplus is retained and invested, it may not improve resilience. Likewise, a growing business may have an impressive valuation while remaining unable to pay its bills.

Next, make an opportunity map. Which skills could improve earning power? Which relationships might provide knowledge rather than favours? What ownership opportunities are available without endangering essential household needs? In markets where access to capital is limited, cooperative investment, credible syndication and regulated pooled vehicles may sometimes broaden participation, but each demands governance and diligence.

Finally, be careful about comparison. Another person's portfolio may conceal leverage, inheritance, concentrated risk or a different time horizon. Your own progress is better judged by whether your financial position becomes more resilient and your choices become less constrained over time.

Recognising unequal starting conditions should also change how we design institutions. Transparent markets, accessible savings products, fair credit assessment and credible investor protection can widen opportunity. Individual discipline matters; so do the systems that determine whose discipline can translate into ownership.

Field note: Measure progress against a sensible personal baseline, not somebody else's highlight reel.

What would you change in your own financial decisions after examining this idea? The point of this series is not to offer a shortcut to wealth, but to make the trade-offs clearer and the decisions more deliberate.
