The Three Types of Luck

Luck is not a single mysterious event. It shapes the starting hand, the opportunities we encounter and the results we receive.

The question behind the outcome

Constitutive luck concerns characteristics and circumstances we did not choose, including the family and environment into which we were born. Circumstantial luck concerns encounters and conditions: a mentor, a market opening or a timely introduction. Outcome luck concerns what happens after a decision, when uncertainty still remains.

What makes this difficult

These categories overlap. An investor may make a well-researched decision yet experience an adverse outcome; another may profit from a weak thesis because a market rally arrived. The result alone does not reveal the quality of the decision.

A more useful way forward

Evaluate decisions with two separate questions: was the process sound given information available at the time, and what happened afterward? Keep a decision journal to separate skill from fortune.

Three ways fortune enters the picture

Constitutive luck is the starting hand. Nobody selects their birthplace, parents, early health or the quality of their first school. These conditions can influence confidence, networks, language, capital access and the ability to take risk. The effects are neither uniform nor destiny, but pretending they do not exist makes financial advice incomplete.

Circumstantial luck is the environment and the encounter. A colleague may introduce a business partner. A regulation may create demand for a new service. A recession may close a market just as a venture becomes operational. We can seek more encounters and study changing markets, but we cannot schedule the precise moment when an opportunity will appear.

Outcome luck is what remains after the decision. An investor can carefully assess cash flows, management and valuation, only to face an unforeseen currency shock. Another investor can make a poorly researched purchase and profit from a broad rally. Neither result, by itself, proves whether the initial process was sound.

Why the distinction matters

Without this distinction, people may mistake favourable outcomes for superior judgement and unfavourable outcomes for personal failure. That is especially dangerous in investment markets, where randomness can reward weak methods for years. A strategy that works during rising prices may reveal its fragility only when liquidity disappears.

The reverse problem also matters. A prudent decision can disappoint. If we abandon every sound process after one poor result, we never learn to distinguish uncertainty from error. The better practice is to judge decisions using the information available at the time, while remaining willing to update the process when new evidence appears.

A practical luck audit

Think about one important financial outcome in your life. Write down the factors you controlled: preparation, spending, research, negotiation or persistence. Then list the conditions you inherited, the circumstances you encountered and the events you could not reasonably predict. Avoid allocating arbitrary percentages; the exercise is about causal humility rather than false precision.

Repeat the audit for a disappointment. Were the assumptions wrong? Was the position too large? Did a genuine external shock occur? Was the risk foreseeable even if its timing was not? These questions produce more useful lessons than the statement that everything happens for a reason.

For investors, a decision journal can preserve the original thesis, expected scenarios, probability ranges and reasons for sizing the position. Review it after six or twelve months. Note where judgement was good but the outcome bad, and where luck concealed weak analysis.

Luck is not a substitute for responsibility. It is a reminder to take responsibility for the decisions we can make while recognising the limits of personal control. That recognition can make us more careful investors and more generous judges of other people's lives.

Field note: Respect luck without surrendering agency. Good processes improve your chances, not your certainty.
