Your Mind Can Make You Poorer

Investment decisions are made by people, not by spreadsheets alone.

The question behind the outcome

Fear of missing out can make a rising price look like evidence. Loss aversion can keep us attached to a failing investment. Anchoring can turn a purchase price into an irrelevant target. Herd behaviour can substitute social proof for analysis, while present bias rewards immediate comfort over distant goals.

What makes this difficult

None of these tendencies disappears because we know their names. Financial professionals experience them too. The practical defence is a repeatable decision process that slows down reactions and makes assumptions visible.

A more useful way forward

Before a significant decision, record the thesis, valuation or affordability logic, downside scenario and conditions that would change your mind. Use waiting periods for impulsive purchases and review outcomes without rewriting the original rationale.

The stories we tell ourselves about money

When an investment rises quickly, it is tempting to believe the market has confirmed our intelligence. When it falls, we may search for reasons that allow us to avoid admitting a mistake. Both responses protect the ego more readily than the portfolio.

Behavioural finance studies the systematic ways in which judgement can depart from the ideal of consistent, evidence-based choice. The point is not that people are irrational all the time. It is that emotions, limited attention and mental shortcuts influence decisions, especially under uncertainty.

Five traps worth recognising

Fear of missing out makes a popular opportunity feel urgent. Urgency can crowd out questions about valuation, liquidity and the possibility of loss. Loss aversion can make selling a disappointing asset feel like admitting defeat, even when the original thesis has failed. Anchoring causes an arbitrary reference point, such as a purchase price, to dominate the assessment of future value.

Herd behaviour encourages imitation because many others appear confident. Yet popularity is not the same as evidence. Present bias places disproportionate weight on immediate consumption and can undermine long-term saving, even when the person genuinely values future independence.

These tendencies can conflict. Someone may chase a fashionable investment because of FOMO, then refuse to sell after its economics deteriorate because of loss aversion. A single transaction can therefore be shaped by several biases at different stages.

Build a process that can disagree with you

Before investing, write a short decision memo. What is the asset? How does it generate cash? What price are you paying relative to a defensible estimate of value? What could permanently impair capital? What evidence would cause you to change your view?

Record alternatives, including doing nothing. A decision to hold cash for a period may be rational when opportunities are unattractive or near-term obligations are high. Waiting is not necessarily indecision.

For material commitments, invite a credible challenge. Ask someone to identify the strongest argument against the transaction. In a team, separate the person presenting the opportunity from the person testing assumptions. Good governance can reduce the influence of charisma and hierarchy.

Review decisions without rewriting history

After the outcome becomes visible, return to the original memo. Were the assumptions supported? Did the downside scenario occur? Was the position appropriately sized? A profitable trade made on weak reasoning should not automatically be celebrated as proof of skill. A loss after a sound, bounded decision should not automatically be treated as evidence of incompetence.

Personal finance benefits from similar guardrails. Automated savings, spending limits and cooling-off periods reduce the number of decisions that must be made under pressure. The best system is one you can maintain when enthusiasm fades.

Financial discipline is not the absence of emotion. It is the practice of ensuring that emotion does not have the final vote on every important decision.

Field note: Discipline is less about possessing a perfect temperament than building a process that anticipates imperfect judgement.

What would you change in your own financial decisions after examining this idea? The point of this series is not to offer a shortcut to wealth, but to make the trade-offs clearer and the decisions more deliberate.
