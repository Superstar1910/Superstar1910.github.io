Optionality and the Investor’s Mindset: Part 1, Lessons from Umeme

What if you could make a bet on the future, one that preserved your downside, but kept the upside open-ended? That’s the quiet appeal of an option, a financial instrument often misunderstood as speculative but, at its core, is a structured way to navigate uncertainty.

Right now, a curious example of this is playing out in Uganda. Umeme, the country’s former electricity distributor, no longer runs any power lines, no longer maintains the grid. And yet, its stock has resumed trading. Investors aren’t buying kilowatts or infrastructure. They are buying the possibility of a payout, tied to a pending legal claim. In other words, they’re buying optionality.

This article is about more than markets, it is about how we price future outcomes, how we live with uncertainty and how the structure of options, financial or otherwise, can help us make smarter bets in life.

What’s an Option?

An option gives you the right, but not the obligation, to benefit from a possible future outcome.

There are two basic types:

Call Option - The Right to Upside

Imagine putting down a deposit to hold a plot of land for one to purchase at a given price for a given period. If the land becomes valuable, you follow through and buy it. If it doesn’t, you walk away, losing only the deposit. This is a land option.

Call options are about keeping access to a favorable future while limiting downside exposure.

This is the nature of Umeme stock today. Its shareholders are not betting on earnings or dividends, they are holding a claim on a legal process, with a capped downside and an uncertain but meaningful upside.

Put Option - The Right to Protection

Now consider the opposite scenario. You already own something say, that same land, and you are unsure about the future value (war, fake title, flooding, land rezoning). You buy a guarantee that lets you sell it back at a pre-agreed price if the value falls.

That is a put option, a hedge against loss. It protects you when things go wrong, even if you hope they won’t.

In everyday life, a put might look like:

  • Travel insurance, life insurance

  • An emergency fund

  • A probationary period on a job

  • A short-term lease before buying property

Both types of options are about navigating risk, one opens a path to reward, the other cushions potential loss.

The Umeme Case: A Real-World Option

In March 2025, Umeme’s 20-year electricity concession ended. The government paid USD 118 million as part of the agreed buyout. But Umeme insists that the total owed is closer to USD 292 million, an additional payment of USD 174 million, and has decided to take the matter to international arbitration.

Umeme Limited was first incorporated in Uganda as a private limited company on May 6, 2004, and later converted into a public company on June 28, 2012 under the Companies Act. Umeme is a subsidiary of Umeme Holdings, which is itself a subsidiary of Actis Infrastructure 2 LP, an investment fund under the management of Actis, through a holding company in Mauritius, Actis Infrastructure Umeme Limited. Umeme took over the distribution system for the distribution and supply of electricity in Uganda from Uganda Electricity Distribution Company Ltd (UEDCL) under the concession for a period of 20 years commencing March 1, 2005.

So what do shareholders really own?

Not a utility, but a legal claim. A financial interest in the outcome of a binding arbitration process. This is what gives the shares value.

At the time of writing this article, Umeme is trading at UGX 415 per share. We shall take the view of an investor who purchased Umeme at UGX 275 per share. To understand options, I will define some of the related terms using Umeme as an analogy of a call option.

  1. Call Option: A call option gives you the right to buy something at a fixed price in the future.

Umeme analogy: As a shareholder, you’ve paid UGX 275 per share to gain exposure to a much bigger payoff - if the arbitration succeeds.

  1. Strike Price: This is the price you must pay to exercise the option - in the land option example above this is the price at which you can buy the land versus its current market price.

Umeme analogy: The strike price is the UGX 275 you paid per share to hold the stock. That’s your upfront cost for the “right” to benefit from the arbitration.

  1. Premium: This is the cost of the option, what you paid for the opportunity - in the land option example above this is the deposit you pay for the right to buy the land at the Strike Price.

Umeme analogy: The premium is also UGX 275 per share, the amount you invested to “lock in” the chance at a big payout from the legal win.

  1. Expiration: The date by which the option must be resolved (or becomes worthless).

Umeme analogy: There’s no exact expiry date, but the arbitration ruling will eventually resolve the claim, likely in 1–2 years. That’s your real-world “expiration.”

  1. Intrinsic Value: This is the value the option has right now, based on current facts.

Umeme analogy: Even if nothing else is paid, Umeme already received USD 118 million, which supports a per-share value of UGX 272. That’s your intrinsic value.

  1. Time Value: The value of the uncertain future payout, how much potential remains as time goes on.

Umeme analogy: The hope of winning USD 174M is the time value. The longer the claim remains alive, the more potential upside you hold.

  1. In the Money: An option is “in the money” if it would already pay off if resolved today.

Umeme analogy: At UGX 275 per share, and with UGX 272 backed by the paid USD 118M, if the legal claim pays out an additional amount say USD 174 million which translates into an additional UGX 401.5 per share you are in the money.

  1. Out of the Money: An option is “out of the money” if it’s not currently worth anything, but could be if conditions change.

Umeme analogy: If you bought at the current price of UGX 415, hoping for a legal win, you’re out of the money today but could win if the full claim is paid.

Options Show Up in Life, Too

This framework isn’t just confined to financial markets. In life, we regularly make choices that resemble options:

  • Enrolling in school is an option on future career paths.

  • Saving and investing early is an option on future freedom.

  • Learning a language or acquiring a skill is an option on geographic or professional flexibility.

  • A mentorship is an option on future perspective and access.

  • Relocating, dating, starting a side project - all these can behave like options.

They often cost something upfront such as time, energy, money but leave room for upside later.

Optionality is the discipline of saying “I don’t need this to pay off now. But I want to keep the chance alive.”

Aligning options with life goals begins with intentionality. Rather than trying to predict the future, focus on positioning yourself to benefit from it. Ask yourself, what outcomes do I value over the next 5, 10, or 20 years? What actions today preserve my ability to pursue those outcomes later even if I’m not ready or certain now? And just as important, where might I already hold valuable options in life: skills, relationships, experiences that I’ve overlooked or underutilized? Like financial options, the key is to protect your downside while keeping the door open to future upside.

Final Thought

Umeme’s story is unusual. But it’s also illustrative. The stock has shifted from a utility play to a legal option. Most investors missed the transition. Some might miss the value. But the lesson runs deeper than that:

The best bets in life aren’t always about being right. Sometimes, they’re about not losing much if you're wrong, and gaining a lot if you're right.

You don’t need to predict the future. But you can position yourself to benefit from it.
