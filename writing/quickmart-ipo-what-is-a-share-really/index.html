Quickmart IPO: What Is a Share Really Worth at KES 7.50?

Originally published on Substack: 2026-10-12 · Original publication ↗

Associated research repository ↗ (linkage to be verified against the article evidence)

By Kenneth S. Legesi, CFA | 12 October 2026

Kenya’s Quickmart IPO presents an interesting opportunity: a profitable supermarket operator with an established national footprint, growing revenues and ambitions to expand further.

The offer price is KES 7.50 per share, implying an equity market capitalisation of KES 30 billion.

At first glance, the numbers are attractive. Quickmart generated KES 50.4 billion in revenue and KES 1.51 billion in net profit in 2025. Management forecasts earnings growth in 2026 and 2027.

But the question for investors is not simply whether Quickmart is a good business.

The question is whether the price being asked adequately compensates investors for the risks of owning it.

I approach this through five questions: Is it a good business? Does it generate cash? Is the valuation reasonable? What exactly are investors buying? And what could go wrong?

The Quickmart IPO at a glance

Before assessing whether Quickmart’s IPO represents an attractive investment, it is important to understand the scale of the business, its financial performance and the terms on which shares are being offered. At KES 7.50 per share, the IPO values Quickmart at KES 30 billion, against FY2025 revenues of KES 50.43 billion and net profit of KES 1.51 billion. Management forecasts further earnings growth in 2026, but investors must distinguish the company’s operating performance from the price they are being asked to pay. The snapshot below summarises the key figures that provide the starting point for our investment assessment.

The last two figures are analytical estimates based on different methodologies, not independently validated price targets.

1. Is Quickmart a good business?

Quickmart has developed a substantial position in Kenya’s organised grocery retail market, operating approximately 72 stores at the time of the offer.

Supermarkets can be attractive businesses when they combine high inventory turnover, efficient procurement, disciplined operating costs and favourable supplier-payment terms.

Customers generally pay immediately, while suppliers may allow retailers time to settle invoices. This can support operating cash flow and enable growth without requiring equivalent increases in shareholder capital.

But supermarket retailing is also competitive and typically operates on relatively thin net margins.

Success depends on store location, product availability, pricing, customer loyalty, inventory management and logistics.

Quickmart’s expansion plans provide opportunities for growth. However, opening additional stores creates value only if the returns generated exceed the cost of the capital invested.

What matters is not simply the number of stores Quickmart opens, but how much economic value each new store creates.

The key operating indicators to monitor after listing include same-store sales growth, gross margins, store-level profitability, inventory turnover, new-store payback periods and return on invested capital.

2. Does Quickmart turn profits into cash?

Quickmart’s FY2025 results show a profitable business with substantial reported operating cash generation.

FY2025 financial performance, KES billion

  • Revenue, 50.43

  • Reported EBITDA, 5.08

  • Adjusted EBITDA, 5.36

  • Profit after tax, 1.51

  • Operating cash flow after tax, 4.83

  • Property and equipment expenditure, 1.09

  • Lease principal repayments, 1.61

  • Dividends paid, 1.65

Operating cash flow exceeded reported net profit by a considerable margin.

That is encouraging, but investors should avoid treating all operating cash flow as freely distributable cash.

Quickmart must maintain existing stores, finance expansion, manage inventory, settle contractual lease obligations and preserve adequate liquidity.

A simplified calculation illustrates the distinction:

KES 4.83bn operating cash flow − KES 1.09bn capital expenditure − KES 1.61bn lease principal = approximately KES 2.13bn.

This is an illustrative cash subtotal, not a fully reconciled measure of free cash flow. It does not by itself establish the cash available to shareholders.

The company also paid approximately KES 1.65 billion in dividends during 2025, compared with net profit of KES 1.51 billion.

Distributing more than one year’s profit is not inherently problematic. A company may have accumulated reserves and sufficient cash resources.

However, a growing retailer must balance dividends against capital expenditure, working-capital needs and financial obligations.

The investment question is whether Quickmart can simultaneously expand, meet its obligations and sustain shareholder distributions without weakening its financial position.

3. Is KES 7.50 a reasonable price?

Valuation is where a good business becomes either an attractive or unattractive investment.

At the IPO offer price, Quickmart is valued at approximately KES 30 billion.

Management forecasts FY2026 profit after tax of approximately KES 2.103 billion.

With 4 billion shares outstanding, forecast earnings per share are:

KES 2.103bn ÷ 4bn = KES 0.526 per share.

At KES 7.50, investors are paying approximately 14.3 times forecast FY2026 reported earnings.

Is that expensive or cheap?

One approach is to compare Quickmart with listed grocery retailers elsewhere. Another is to estimate the present value of the cash the business could generate.

Neither method should be used without considering its limitations.

The earnings-multiple approach

Applying an illustrative 13x forward P/E multiple gives:

KES 0.526 × 13 = KES 6.83 per share.

This is approximately 9% below the IPO offer price.

The 13x multiple is a screening assumption. It reflects a judgmental discount to international peers for differences in market risk, company scale, liquidity and operating characteristics. It is not a statistically validated fair-value multiple.

This illustrates an important principle: a valuation multiple is an expression of expectations and risk, not a universal rule.

What do international grocery retailers trade at?

Stanbic Bank Kenya/SBG Securities’ September 2026 valuation parameters include 11 listed grocery retailers across South Africa, Morocco, Egypt, Turkey and Gulf markets.

The sample’s median FY2026 forecast P/E is approximately 17.7x, with a median EV/EBITDA of 8.9x.

Quickmart’s 14.3x forward reported P/E is below that international median.

That may suggest an attractive entry price, but the comparison needs interpretation.

Retailers in different countries face different financing costs, inflation, currency risks, consumer purchasing power and stock-market liquidity.

They may also have different store formats, margins, growth expectations and accounting policies.

A direct application of the 17.7x peer median would imply approximately KES 9.31 per Quickmart share. But that does not mean Quickmart necessarily deserves that valuation.

A 20% discount to the median, for example, would imply approximately KES 7.45 per share, close to the IPO price.

That discount is illustrative, not an independently established adjustment.

The conclusion from comparables is that Quickmart is priced below the international peer median, but whether the discount is sufficient remains an open question.

4. What does a discounted cash-flow valuation suggest?

An earnings multiple tells us how the market values comparable profits.

A discounted cash-flow model attempts to answer a different question: What is the present value of the cash Quickmart could generate in the future?

The provisional DCF therefore indicates approximately 15.4% potential upside to the KES 7.50 offer price.

However, this result requires several qualifications.

First, approximately 55% of enterprise value comes from terminal value—the value attributed to cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period.

Second, the 16% WACC and 4% perpetual growth rate are analytical assumptions that still require complete market calibration.

Third, future cash flows depend on management delivering its forecasts, maintaining margins and generating appropriate returns on reinvestment.

Fourth, the treatment of lease obligations requires further reconciliation.

Why the lease treatment matters

Quickmart has substantial contractual lease commitments associated with its store network.

Under IFRS 16, many lease obligations are recognised on the balance sheet, while their costs affect the financial statements differently from conventional operating rental expenses.

An alternative rental-based valuation cross-check indicates approximately KES 6.75 per share, compared with KES 8.65 from the revised enterprise DCF.

The difference of approximately KES 1.90 per share is material.

The rental-based cross-check uses assumptions that have not yet been fully reconciled to contractual lease schedules. It is therefore not a validated lower valuation bound.

A complete valuation should consistently account for lease payments, renewals, interest, new-store leases and the financial obligations deducted from enterprise value.

The cost of capital also needs to be supported by market-observed long-term Kenyan interest rates, an appropriate equity risk premium, comparable-company risk and actual financing costs.

The KES 8.65 DCF result is therefore an indicative scenario—not a definitive intrinsic-value target.

5. What exactly are investors buying?

The IPO structure is particularly important.

Quickmart is offering 2 billion existing shares, representing 50% of its issued share capital, through a secondary sale by Sokoni Retail Kenya Limited.

At KES 7.50 per share, a fully subscribed offer would generate approximately KES 15 billion in gross proceeds for the selling shareholder.

Quickmart itself receives no new equity capital from the IPO.

This does not automatically make the transaction unattractive.

Secondary offerings can create liquidity for existing shareholders, broaden ownership and establish a publicly traded market for the company.

But the distinction matters for investors evaluating future growth.

If Quickmart intends to expand its store network, it must finance that expansion through internally generated cash, borrowing, supplier financing or other sources—not through the proceeds of this IPO.

Investors should therefore consider the company’s dividend policy, capital allocation priorities, debt and lease obligations, related-party transactions and the incentives of its continuing shareholders.

A listing also creates expectations around governance, transparency and financial reporting.

These factors can materially influence long-term investment returns.

Quickmart appears to be an established, profitable and growing supermarket operator. Its commercial proposition is credible, and its earnings forecasts indicate potential for further expansion.

But at KES 7.50, the investment case is not conclusively attractive on the information currently validated.

The revised DCF suggests upside, while the more conservative earnings approach indicates downside. The outstanding lease reconciliation and discount-rate calibration are material to the conclusion.

My assessment: WATCH / DEFER

I would want stronger evidence on sustainable cash generation, lease economics, capital allocation and returns from new stores before concluding that the IPO offers a sufficient margin of safety.

That is not a prediction that the shares will fall after listing. Market prices can diverge substantially from analytical estimates, particularly around an IPO.

It is a statement about the evidence needed to make a disciplined investment decision.

A good business is not necessarily a good investment at any price. What matters is the relationship between price, future cash flows and risk.
