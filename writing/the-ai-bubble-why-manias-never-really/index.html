The AI Bubble: Why Manias Never Really Die (Part 1)

Originally published on Substack: 2025-10-12 · Original publication ↗

Disclaimer: This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute investment advice. Please consult your financial advisor before making any investment decisions.

The Spark Before the Storm

I am currently reading The Nature of Technology: What It Is and How It Evolves by W. Brian Arthur, it is a slow but enjoyable read. I agree that it is one of the most insightful books ever written on how technologies emerge, combine, and transform economies and how those domains in turn reshape the economy.

One section that particularly caught my attention is the interplay between technology and finance: how every technological breakthrough needs its financial counterpart to scale, how capital markets act as midwives of innovation, and how enthusiasm can spill into speculation. This concept is expounded on further by Carlota Perez as described in her 2003 book, Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital: The Dynamics of Bubbles and Golden Ages.

That reflection naturally led me to AI, the technology du jour shaping both our imagination and our investments. It made me wonder: if every past technological leap, from railways to the internet, has sparked both transformation and mania, can those lessons guide us through the present moment?

This week’s markets offered a hint. The Nasdaq slipped roughly 2%, AI leaders swung 10% intraday, and yet conversations still orbit one word, AI. In the markets, the excitement is palpable; so is the unease. Everyone wants exposure, but no one wants risk.

It’s been that way for the past few years. Since late 2022, when the first generation of large language models captured public attention, AI-linked equities have dominated returns. The semiconductor index has more than tripled, the major AI platform stocks have risen five- to ten-fold, and a handful of names now account for over 30% of the S&P 500’s total market value1. Yet beneath those headlines lies sharp volatility: 10% daily swings, 30–40% corrections in single names, and repeated rotations as investors question whether earnings can keep pace with expectations.

See from The Kobeitsi Letter2 commentary on the performance of the Semiconductors Index, relative to the S&P 500 equal-weighted index which reached 0.86, the highest since the 2000 Dot-Com Bubble.

Even outside the technology sector, markets have been pulled into AI’s gravitational field: utilities rallying on data-centre power demand, industrials on cooling and grid equipment, and real estate on hyperscale land. Every portfolio seems to carry a trace of the theme.

“Every generation thinks it has invented a new way to make money fast. It hasn’t.”
Niall Ferguson, The Ascent of Money

History tells us that moments like these, when optimism meets over-extension, often feel euphoric right before they turn instructive. The challenge for investors isn’t spotting the excitement; it’s distinguishing durable transformation from temporary mania.

How do we tell when genuine innovation crosses into mania and what does a wise investor do about it?

The Anatomy of a Mania

1. The Pattern That Never Changes

History doesn’t repeat, but it often rhymes, especially where money meets invention.
Every great wave of technology has begun with a spark of discovery, grown into a blaze of optimism, and ended in the ashes of overreach. Yet each left behind a stronger, more connected, more capable world.

The Railway Mania (1840s)

The first great industrial mania began with steam. Britain’s railway revolution promised to collapse distances and fuel commerce. Between 1843 and 1847, the number of miles of track approved by Parliament tripled. Investors rushed in — shopkeepers, farmers, aristocrats — everyone owned “railway scrip.”

Railway scrip was a tradable promise of participation in the railway revolution—an early, paper-based version of speculative tech equity. It fueled the dreams (and disasters) of the 1840s boom, much like startup shares and AI tokens do in our own age.

When profits failed to match expectations, credit tightened, and the bubble burst. Shares fell as much as 85%, fortunes vanished, and projects were abandoned mid-construction. Yet, within a decade, the same tracks underpinned Victorian industrial dominance. The frenzy, though ruinous to many investors, had financed the very arteries of modern trade.

The Roaring Twenties (1920s)

The 1920s combined electricity, cars, and mass production: the technological trinity that defined modern life. The U.S. economy surged, and the Dow Jones rose sixfold in less than a decade. Ordinary citizens bought stocks on margin, convinced that a “new era” of permanent prosperity had begun.

But behind the exuberance lay fragile foundations: slowing productivity, speculative credit, and a market decoupled from earnings. The 1929 crash erased nearly 90% of the Dow’s value and ushered in the Great Depression. Yet the electrification and manufacturing capacity built in that decade became the backbone of mid-century prosperity.

The Internet Boom (1990s)

The dot-com era was the next great act of optimism. Between 1995 and 2000, the Nasdaq soared eightfold as investors imagined a limitless digital economy. Companies with no profits, sometimes no products, achieved billion-dollar valuations on the promise of “eyeballs” and “clicks.”
When reality intruded, the market collapsed by 78%. But the infrastructure survived: the fiber-optic cables, servers, and networking protocols financed during the boom made cloud computing, e-commerce, and digital payments possible. The wreckage of the mania became the foundation of the modern internet economy.

Crypto and Electric Vehicles (2017–2021)

The cycle repeated with digital assets and clean energy. Bitcoin rose twentyfold in a single year; new tokens appeared daily; valuations detached from utility. Electric-vehicle makers enjoyed trillion-dollar market caps before achieving consistent profits.
When rates rose and liquidity drained, speculative tokens vanished and EV shares fell 60–80%. Yet blockchain technology continues to evolve quietly in payments and supply chains, while electric mobility is reshaping transport and energy. The crash again purified excess and redirected capital toward real progress.

Economic historian Carlota Perez argues that every major technological revolution follows a recognizable four-part rhythm. From the Industrial Revolution to the Internet Age, the same sequence repeats — only the names and tools change.

Stage 1: Irruption - The Breakthrough (2017 - 2020)

Every revolution starts quietly, with a technical leap that seems academic at first.

For AI, this began with the Transformer architecture introduced by Google in 2017, the foundation for models like GPT, Claude, Gemini and others. Suddenly, machines could handle not just data but language, reasoning and creativity.

These breakthroughs built on decades of progress in neural networks, cheap cloud computing and open-source collaboration. In Perez’s terms, this was the irruption phase - the spark that revealed a new technological frontier.

At this stage, adoption was limited to research labs and specialized developers. Yet the groundwork was being laid: cloud infrastructure from Amazon, Microsoft and Google; GPUs from Nvidia; and vast datasets that would fuel machine intelligence.

Stage 2: Frenzy - The Capital Rush (2023 - 2025)

The frenzy phase is when finance meets imagination. Speculative capital floods in, convinced it has found the future, and for once, it’s not entirely wrong.

We entered this phase in 2023. ChatGPT’s viral adoption triggered a gold rush. Nvidia became a trillion-dollar company; OpenAI secured a record-breaking $40 billion funding round3, Anthropic is aiming for up to $10 billion in new capital4 and corporate AI investment globally has already exceeded $250 billion annually5.

This year, data center construction has exploded. Microsoft, Amazon and Google each announced $80–100 billion capex plans, while countries from Japan to the UAE declared national AI missions. Venture funding in AI startups is at record highs, and even non-tech firms now label themselves “AI-powered” to capture investor attention.

But as with every frenzy before such as railways, radio and internet, valuations have detached from fundamentals. GPU shortages, model training costs and power constraints are surfacing as the limits of optimism6.

This is where manias begin to rhyme. Perez calls this “the turning of the tide,” when excess money builds the roads for future prosperity, even as it sets up the eventual correction.

Stage 3: Turning Point - The Correction (2026 - 2027?)

In every cycle, the frenzy outruns reality. Markets reach a point where the marginal dollar of investment produces less excitement and more scrutiny789, for example:

  • According to Bloomberg Intelligence AI Infrastructure Report 2025, hyperscaler capex has grown ~50% YoY, yet projected AI revenue contribution for 2025–26 has risen by less than 20% - this implies a declining marginal return per invested dollar.

  • Per Goldman Sachs Research – AI Economics Deep Dive 2025, Analysts estimate the AI infrastructure payback horizon has extended to 5–7 years, up from 3–4 years expected in 2023

For AI, this inflection may arrive when infrastructure growth outpaces near-term monetization, when data centers are built faster than they can be filled with profitable workloads. Costs will collide with competition, open-source models will undercut proprietary ones, margins will compress and investors will suddenly rediscover valuation discipline.

The turning point isn’t necessarily catastrophic; it is the system’s reset. Many early players will fail or consolidate, while survivors with genuine advantages in areas such as chips, cloud scale, power access, and proprietary data will emerge stronger.

If Perez’s framework holds, 2026 to 2027 could resemble the 2001 to 2003 period that followed the dot-com boom, a painful but ultimately productive reset. Then, as now, the world had overbuilt infrastructure ahead of demand: too many websites, too much fiber-optic capacity, and too little profit to justify valuations. The crash cleared the field for durable businesses like Amazon and Google to emerge and for broadband and cloud computing to take hold.

In the same way, a future AI correction may feel severe in the moment with funding drying up, valuations compressing and speculative projects fading. Yet, it would also set the stage for a decade of genuine deployment and productivity gains as AI becomes embedded across industries.

Stage 4: Synergy - The Integration (Late 2020s and Beyond)

After the storm comes the synthesis. AI will stop being a separate story and become part of everything: industry, finance, healthcare, logistics, education.

This is when productivity gains materialize. Think of AI copilots built into office software, autonomous manufacturing systems, or energy grids optimized by predictive models. AI won’t feel like “AI” anymore; it will simply be how things work.

This synergy phase is quieter but far more valuable. The internet had its own version after 2003: e-commerce, cloud computing and digital payments flourished long after the dot-com crash.

The Deeper Truth

Manias, Perez reminds us, aren’t random explosions of greed. They are the financing mechanism of progress — the messy but necessary overreaction that builds the infrastructure the world later depends on.

The 1840s railway mania financed the tracks that powered 19th-century trade. The 1920s electrification bubble wired the modern home. The 1990s dot-com boom paid for the fiber-optic cables that made cloud computing possible.

Today’s AI boom is doing the same — building the data centers, chips, and energy systems that will form the nervous system of the next economy.

Investors may pay the price upfront, but society reaps the benefit over decades.

Speculative excess is not an accident but the mechanism through which technological revolutions finance the infrastructure of the future. Each cycle ends with over-investment, but that over-investment lays the tracks — literal or digital — for the next age.

2. Historical Echoes

We now stand amid another frenzy. The rise of generative AI through large language models, image synthesis and machine reasoning has triggered a global race to compute, connect and capitalize.
AI’s rally began with the release of transformer-based models in 2017, but true ignition came in late 2022 when chatbots and copilots entered the mainstream. Since then, the semiconductor index has more than tripled; the largest AI platform stocks have multiplied five- to tenfold; and a handful of companies now represent more than a quarter of major indices.

The scale of investment is staggering. Big Tech’s combined AI capital expenditure exceeds $350 billion a year10.

  • Microsoft plans to spend roughly $80 billion on AI data centers.

  • Amazon and Google are in the $100 billion range.

  • Meta is targeting $66–72 billion, with similar spending projected for 2026.

  • TSMC, the world’s largest chip foundry, expects $38–42 billion in annual capex.

  • Equinix and Digital Realty, the “landlords of the internet,” each invest over $3 billion a year to expand data-center capacity.

  • Even governments, from Japan to the UAE, are launching sovereign AI infrastructure funds.

This isn’t vaporware. It’s real money building a new physical internet: high-voltage substations, cooling towers, silicon fabs, and server farms. But markets being markets, optimism has already raced ahead of earnings. AI start-ups command multi-billion valuations on speculative revenue and secondary markets trade equity in companies that have yet to turn a profit.

Just as in previous cycles, the best technologies may still host the worst investments when price outruns profit. The market may be financing the future, but it’s doing so with borrowed time and leveraged expectations.

3. The Mania Mindset

At the heart of every boom is a recurring pattern of psychology that technology merely amplifies.

  • Overconfidence Bias: “This time it’s different.” Investors believe they are smarter, data is richer, and the market has evolved beyond the old rules.

  • FOMO: “I can’t afford to miss it.” Social proof becomes a trading signal and every headline confirms the narrative.

  • Narrative Fallacy: “If it’s a great story, it must be a great investment.” Investors conflate technological possibility with financial inevitability.

  • Liquidity and Leverage: Easy money acts as fuel. Inexpensive credit and abundant venture funding magnify every impulse.

Today’s AI boom is showing the same warning signs that have marked the final stages of every major market frenzy. Venture investors are pouring record sums into a few headline AI startups, driving valuations far beyond what these companies currently earn. In early 2025 alone, AI firms raised more than $70 billion, nearly 60% of all global venture funding11. At the same time, many tech companies are rebranding their products as “AI-powered” to attract attention and fresh capital12. Meanwhile, just a handful of giants; Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, Google, Nvidia, Meta, and Tesla now make up about 30% of the S&P 500’s total value, meaning most of the market’s gains come from a very small group of stocks.

In short, money, hype, and excitement are all crowding into the same few names, a familiar pattern seen before in every great boom from railways to dot-coms.

At a fundamental level, we are witnessing a capital-intensive transformation — data centers, chips, and power infrastructure are the railroads of our age. Each generation of investors finances the next generation of progress, whether it’s steel and steam or silicon and syntax.

But history offers a warning: when enthusiasm becomes uniform and expectations become exponential, even transformative technologies can experience brutal corrections.

Where might we be in the cycle?

Looking across the data, market behavior and capital flows, the evidence suggests that AI has moved firmly into the late-frenzy phase of Carlota Perez’s technological cycle: the period when excitement and capital peak, reality begins to intrude and the seeds of the next reset are sown.

  1. The Build-Out is Real, and Extraordinary

The numbers speak for themselves. Global AI infrastructure spending has entered an unprecedented phase of acceleration:

  • Microsoft is guiding roughly $80 billion in AI and data-center investment this fiscal year.

  • Amazon and Google are each expected to spend around $100 billion in 2025, while Meta plans $66–72 billion.

  • TSMC’s foundry capex remains near record highs at $38–42 billion, and data-center landlords such as Equinix and Digital Realty are each investing more than $3 billion annually.

  • Global electricity demand is rising faster than forecast as AI data centers compete with households and industry for power.

This is the installation stage in full force, capital spending that lays the physical groundwork for a future economy of intelligent machines. It is real, tangible and transformative. Yet, as in every previous technological boom, the market has begun discounting years of future cash flows today.

  1. Market Behaviour Shows Late-Cycle Traits

Valuations have expanded faster than earnings. The semiconductor index has tripled since 2022 and a small cluster of mega-cap AI leaders now contributes over 30 percent of total S&P 500 market capitalization. Equal-weighted stock indexes are falling behind the main market averages, which means that only a few big companies are driving most of the gains. This kind of narrow leadership is a classic sign that the market is in the late, overheated stage of a boom.

Daily volatility in AI names exceeds 8–10 percent, even as the overall volatility index (VIX) drifts in the mid-teens — a sign of complacency at the index level despite turbulence underneath. Investors are still confident, but positioning is crowded, and expectations are stretched.

  1. Strains are Emerging beneath the Surface

Every mania runs into its own limits. The cracks beneath the AI boom are becoming visible in four key areas — supply, power, talent, and investor sentiment.

  • Supply bottlenecks: Nvidia’s H100 and H200 chips remain on allocation with 6–9 month wait times, and high-bandwidth memory suppliers like SK Hynix and Samsung are sold out through 2025, despite ramping output 60 %. Even ASML, the Dutch company that makes the world’s most advanced chipmaking machines, has a record order backlog of more than €39 billion. These EUV (extreme ultraviolet) systems are essential for producing the high-performance chips that power AI, and demand for them far exceeds supply. In short, the world can’t build new compute capacity fast enough to keep up with the pace of AI expansion.

  • Power constraints: Global data-center electricity use is projected to double from 460 TWh in 2022 to more than 1,000 TWh by 2026. In Virginia’s “Data-Center Alley,” grid-connection requests have surged 250 % since 2022, with five- to seven-year wait times. Ireland and Singapore have paused new permits to protect strained grids.

  • Human capital: Demand for AI talent grew 40 % YoY in 2024 while supply rose only 14 %. U.S. senior ML engineers now command $450 k+ total pay, up 30–40 % since 2022, as firms like OpenAI and Meta bid millions to retain scarce expertise.

  • Investor fatigue: The semiconductor index has tripled since 2022, while sector earnings are up only 60 %. The “Magnificent 7” make up about 30 % of S&P 500 value, yet equal-weighted returns are flat. Even Nvidia’s 20 % earnings beat in August 2025 saw its stock fall 7 %.

Together, these numbers tell the story of an industry running hot, real progress colliding with physical, human and financial limits. These are not fatal flaws. They are the natural growing pains that mark the transition from pure optimism to practical execution.

  1. Why “Late-Frenzy”?

The metrics suggest that the market that has overshot fundamentals but not yet broken down, the precise inflection where enthusiasm meets friction.

  1. Interpreting the Moment

If Perez’s framework holds, 2026–2027 could mirror the 2001–2003 post–dot-com adjustment, a painful but necessary reset. Then, as now, over-investment built the digital plumbing such as fiber-optic cables and server farms that enabled a decade of real productivity growth.

A similar process is unfolding today. The world is building the computational, power and connectivity backbone for an AI-driven economy. Some capital will be misallocated; some companies will fail. Yet the infrastructure, and the eventual productivity gains, will endure.

In short, we’re between excitement and fatigue. Investors still believe in the promise of AI, but we’re starting to see the limits - where high prices will soon need real profits to back them up. The progress is real, the money flowing in is huge, yet the early signs of pressure are starting to show.

Final Thoughts

We have reached the point in the cycle where excitement must give way to execution. This is when investors, policymakers and builders need to shift from announcing the future to quietly building it profitably. The AI mania isn’t ending — it’s evolving, moving from euphoria to endurance.

In Part 2: Navigating the Mania, I will explore how investors can keep their footing through the turbulence, how to stay confident but cautious, identify the true long-term winners and use market swings as opportunities rather than threats.

Because while manias may fuel progress, it’s discipline, not hype, that decides who lasts.
