The Gem Inside the Rump

Originally published on Substack: 2026-08-26 · Original publication ↗

Associated research repository ↗ (linkage to be verified against the article evidence)

In 2019, if you had asked any analyst covering East African equities to describe Car and General Kenya, they would have told you the same thing: a Nairobi-listed motor vehicle distributor with colonial-era roots, modest margins, thin coverage, and a share price that had drifted sideways for most of the decade.

They would have been right. And they would have missed everything.

Because inside Car and General, something was being built that had almost nothing to do with selling motorbikes. A motorcycle financing startup called Watu Credit was quietly rewriting the rules of asset-backed lending across the continent — not through venture capital, not through a Silicon Valley pitch deck, but through Car and General’s commercial infrastructure, its dealer networks, and its deep East African market knowledge. And then out through six countries and half a billion dollars in disbursements.

Car and General’s share price never got the memo.

The Architecture of a Mispricing

Markets are precise about some things and systematically wrong about others. They are precise about cash flows they can model. They are systematically wrong about value that is hidden, structural, or requires a different mental model than the one the market applies to the visible business.

Car and General trades as a motor vehicle distributor because that is what it has always been. That label determines which analysts cover it, which comparable companies they use, and what multiple it receives. The label is not wrong. It is incomplete.

Watu Credit is not a motor vehicle story. It is a fintech story. It uses proprietary credit scoring models trained on asset behavior and mobile money repayment history — data no bank in East Africa possesses at this scale. It finances motorcycles, three-wheelers, and smartphones on lease-to-own terms for borrowers who have never held a formal bank account. It has disbursed more than USD 500 million across Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, Ivory Coast, and beyond. A pure-play fintech with Watu Credit’s characteristics would not trade at a distribution-company multiple. In a world where African fintech comparables trade at 4–8× revenue, Watu Credit’s standalone value is not zero. But that is approximately what Car and General’s share price implies.

This gap — between what a company appears to be and what it actually contains — is the Embedded Call Option.

The Vocabulary

The series introduction established why this framework needed naming. Here is the vocabulary that makes it operational.

The Rump is the mature core business. It is what analysts model. It is what most investors are paying for. It generates cash, employs most of the staff, and dominates the income statement. It is real and valuable — but it is, in the relevant sense, already priced.

The Gem is the embedded asset. It is what the market is not pricing — or pricing badly. It may be a fintech subsidiary, a digital platform, an AI application, a data business, or a minority stake in something growing at 40% per year. The Gem is undervalued for structural reasons: too small to move the parent’s valuation metrics, obscured by the Rump’s accounting, or below the scale at which analysts begin to model it seriously.

The Embedded Call Option is the asymmetric payoff structure that results. The investor acquires the Rump at its market-implied value and receives the Gem at a fraction of what it would trade at if listed independently. If the Gem unlocks — through a spin-off, an IPO, a strategic sale, or a re-rating — the payoff is non-linear. If it never unlocks, the investor still holds the Rump at a reasonable price. The downside is bounded by the Rump. The upside is determined by the Gem.

The Telecom Hiding a Bank

MTN Uganda has operated in Uganda since 1998. It sells airtime, data bundles, and postpaid contracts. It competes with Airtel. Its peers are other African telcos. The market prices it accordingly.

But MTN Uganda also owns MTN MoMo — Mobile Money — which processes transactions worth more than Uganda’s entire formal banking system’s annual throughput. MoMo has over 18 million registered users in Uganda alone. Across the MTN Group’s 19 markets, the MoMo platform has approximately 72 million active users. It is a payment processor, a savings wallet, a lending platform, an insurance distribution channel, and — for millions of Ugandans who have never held a formal bank account — a financial identity system.

A pure-play digital financial services company with MoMo’s scale and growth rate would attract a 15–25× revenue multiple in any rational comparable set. MTN Uganda trades at a blended telecom-fintech multiple that applies telecom pricing to the entire enterprise. MoMo — the Gem — is included at whatever residual the market assigns after pricing the telecom. In practice, that residual is a fraction of standalone value.

“The market prices the label. The investor with a different lens prices the architecture.”

Why This Is Structural, Not Accidental

Analysts cover sectors, not structures. A telecom analyst benchmarks MTN Uganda against Airtel Uganda and Safaricom. The fintech business does not appear in the comparable set. The fintech analyst, conversely, does not cover listed telcos. The Gem falls between coverage silos by design.

Standard valuation frameworks are backward-looking. They discount what is known. A business with venture-like upside embedded inside a conglomerate discount has a payoff profile that spreadsheets systematically understate. And if MoMo generates 25% of group revenue but is growing at 45%, it still cannot move consolidated EPS meaningfully when the telecom business is four times its size. The analyst who covers EPS does not have a reason to update the model for a subsidiary too small to change the bottom line.

The result is systematic. Persistent. And exploitable by investors willing to read the architecture rather than the label.

The Payoff Structure

The asymmetry is in the first two rows. The bear case requires genuine deterioration of the core business, not merely the Gem failing to unlock. An investor who correctly prices the Rump at or below fair value has bounded the bear case at a manageable level.

“Sometimes the most valuable part of a company is the part investors don’t yet recognize.”

SOTP Valuation Walkthrough: MTN Uganda

Expected return: potentially mid-to-high teens annually, including dividends, if MoMo value is progressively unlocked. The telecom business provides the cash-flow foundation; MoMo provides upside optionality.
