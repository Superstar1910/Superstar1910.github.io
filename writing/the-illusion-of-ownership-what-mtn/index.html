The Illusion of Ownership: What MTN Uganda’s MoMo Restructure Means for Investors

Originally published on Substack: 2025-06-16 · Original publication ↗

Associated research repository ↗ (linkage to be verified against the article evidence)

In a previous article, The Untold Story Behind Africa’s Most Valuable Fintech Spinout, I explored how MTN has built one of Africa’s most powerful digital finance businesses and why its value often goes unnoticed by everyday investors.

This follow-up discusses a related and expected development: MTN Uganda’s proposal to separate its Mobile Money business (MoMo) into a new company.

This article unpacks what’s at stake, especially for MTN Uganda’s minority shareholders, and why this decision could shape how future fintech (or high growth assets) value is shared, or not, across Africa’s public markets. It also raises an important question, the kind that matters for anyone investing in African markets:

Do investors really own what they think they own?

What MTN Uganda Is Proposing

For context, MTN Uganda has called an Extraordinary General Meeting (EGM) on July 2, 2025 to approve a major structural separation of its mobile money arm (MTN MoMo).

As has been shared in the Shareholder Circular, ahead of the EGM, the crux of the vote:

  • MTN MoMo (U) Ltd will be merged into a new entity, MTN New FinCo, which is licensed by BoU.

  • Ownership of New FinCo will be:

    • 76.015% MTN Group Fintech Holdings B.V.

    • 23.985% via a Trust for MTN Uganda minority shareholders

  • Dividends from MTN New FinCo will flow through the Trust to minority shareholders.

  • The Trust is temporary, with a 3–5 year horizon, after which MTN New FinCo may be listed.

At a glance, this looks clean and compliant.

But the deeper issue? The shareholder disappears.

This is a pivotal corporate event, not just for MTN Uganda, but for how African capital markets handle the ownership of high-growth assets like fintech. It’s a real-time case study of a question:

How can investors in African (frontier) markets extract the most return per unit of risk and actually retain control of the value they help create?

Too often, the answer is: they can’t. Because while they may appear to own a stake, the underlying structure tells a different story one where control, transparency, and access to upside quietly slip away.

The Disappearing Shareholder

The proposed structure effectively sidelines MTN Uganda’s minority shareholders.

First, it replaces direct ownership with indirect exposure. Investors no longer own a piece of the mobile money business itself, instead, they hold a claim via a trust, with no voting rights, no board representation, and no control over key decisions.

Second, there is no guaranteed liquidity. While there is mention of a future listing for MTN New FinCo, there is no fixed timeline, no legal commitment, and no mechanism ensuring that trust units will be converted into listed equity. Investors are being asked to wait indefinitely without clarity on when or how they can access the value they’re promised.

Third, the structure offers no assurance of performance transparency. If the FinCo remains unlisted for years, there is no obligation to publish detailed financials. Yet this business will likely be one of the most valuable and fastest-growing component of the MTN ecosystem in Uganda.

This isn’t true ownership. It’s synthetic exposure, a placeholder asset governed by someone else’s discretion. It’s the capital markets equivalent of being invited to the party, but locked in the lobby while the real celebration happens behind closed doors.

We have seen this before

This is the illusion many retail and institutional investors in frontier markets live under. We have seen it before in Tigo Tanzania’s unfulfilled IPO, Jumia’s offshore listing in New York, Ethiopia’s exclusion of bank investors, Dangote Cement’s opaque related-party dynamics and Zambia’s ZCCM-IH’s role as a policy vehicle, not a value driver.

The pattern is clear, exposure without control is not ownership. And without ownership, risk becomes a one-way street. You may be promised returns, but if you can’t vote, can’t see the books, and can’t exit then you don’t truly own the asset. You are exposed to the downside but locked out of the upside. That’s not investing. That’s carrying risk without any control over how it is managed or shared.

The examples from Nigeria, Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Zambia reveal a recurring pattern in frontier markets, retail and institutional investors are often given the illusion of ownership without meaningful rights. Whether through offshore listings, opaque trust structures, or related-party governance, these models systematically separate investors from the true engines of value creation. The result is limited control, poor transparency, and restricted access to liquidity or influence. These cases underscore a crucial lesson: capital formation is not just about access to returns, but about trust, visibility, and alignment. For markets like Uganda, how minority shareholders are treated today will shape the depth, credibility, and inclusiveness of public markets tomorrow.

True ownership means having a say, having transparency, and having a choice. Without those, you're not a shareholder you're just a bystander.

Why This Matters

1. For Uganda’s Capital Markets
If Uganda aspires to build deeper, more inclusive capital markets, we must embrace a simple principle, risk must be paired with rights. Investors who commit capital should be rewarded not just with potential returns, but with the tools to protect and grow their stake namely, visibility, voice, and exit. When structures obscure ownership or delay access to value, they erode trust and reduce the incentives for long-term participation.

2. For Africa’s Fintech Future
MTN MoMo is arguably Africa’s most scaled and systemically important fintech platform. Its growth has been driven not just by technology, but by infrastructure, user adoption, and regulatory goodwill across markets like Uganda. Yet under the proposed structure, most of that value accrues to MTN Group, while local investors are locked out of both governance and future upside. If this becomes the norm, where local markets host the risk but not the reward, it sets a dangerous precedent for how African fintech is financed and owned.

A Better Way Forward

There is an alternative that balances regulatory compliance with shareholder rights and delivers fairness, transparency, and long-term market integrity.

MTN Uganda could directly allocate shares in FinCo to existing shareholders, held in escrow if necessary. The new entity should commit to a USE listing within a defined timeframe, publish quarterly financial disclosures, and embed strong governance safeguards, including pre-emptive rights for existing shareholders on any future equity issuances or exits.

This approach would still satisfy Bank of Uganda’s regulatory ring-fencing requirements while preserving the trust and participation of investors.

Final Thought

If this proposal moves forward as-is, MTN Uganda’s minority shareholders will be left with exposure without control, income without voice, and value without a vote. That’s not real investing, that’s waiting in the dark while someone else decides your outcome.

This moment is bigger than one company or one EGM. It’s about how African markets choose to handle the value created by their most promising sectors. And for investors, especially in fintech and other high-growth sectors, it is a reminder that structure matters as much as strategy.

You shouldn’t just ask, “Is there upside?” You should ask:

  • Can I exit?

  • Can I see the numbers?

  • Can I influence what happens next?

Because without transparency, governance, and optionality, all you’re left with is risk without agency.
