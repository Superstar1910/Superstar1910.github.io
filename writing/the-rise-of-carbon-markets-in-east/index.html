The Rise of Carbon Markets in East Africa: Opportunities and Challenges

Originally published on Substack: 2025-04-10 · Original publication ↗

🔁 This article is part of a 10-part series on Carbon Markets in East Africa—a deep dive into climate finance, opportunity, and action across the region. To read the introduction to the series and the full article lineup, see here.

Introduction

As the global climate crisis deepens, carbon markets have become one of the most promising tools for mobilizing finance toward climate action. Carbon markets allow countries, companies, and organizations to buy and sell carbon credits. They help put a price on carbon emissions, encouraging businesses and countries to reduce their climate impact. Properly designed, they help reduce emissions where it's most cost-effective while channeling resources to communities and countries that need them the most.

Carbon credits are permits that represent the reduction or removal of one tonne of carbon dioxide (CO₂) or its equivalent in other greenhouse gases. They are used to: 1) Offset emissions by companies, governments, or individuals who want to reduce their climate impact; and 2) Support projects like reforestation, renewable energy, or clean cooking that help cut or absorb emissions. For example, if a company emits 1,000 tonnes of CO₂ but buys 1,000 carbon credits from a forest protection project, it is considered "carbon neutral" for that amount.

East Africa finds itself in a unique and urgent position. The region contributes only a small fraction to global GHG emissions (less than 3%), yet it is among the most vulnerable to climate impacts such as droughts, floods, and shifting agricultural seasons. According to the ND-GAIN Index (2024), East African countries such as Burundi, Uganda, and Rwanda rank among the bottom third globally in climate vulnerability—out of 182 countries assessed. At the same time, many East African countries rank low on human development indicators, out of 193 countries assessed, and face serious economic challenges. Despite these pressures, they have committed to ambitious emissions reduction targets under their nationally determined contributions (NDCs)—often relying on international finance to deliver on these goals.

The table below illustrates this climate paradox: low emissions, high vulnerability, and bold commitments. It underscores why carbon markets in East Africa are not just financial tools—they are also a matter of equity, climate justice, and shared global responsibility.

Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) are each country's official climate action plan under the Paris Agreement. They explain: 1) How much the country plans to reduce its greenhouse gas emissions; 2) What actions it will take to adapt to climate change; and 3) What support (like finance or technology) it might need to meet these goals. Every country sets its own targets and updates them every five years to reflect more ambition over time.

For the region, carbon markets offer a dual opportunity: to attract vital climate finance and to support a transition toward low-carbon, climate-resilient development. However, despite increasing global demand and local enthusiasm, East Africa’s carbon market ecosystem remains young, fragmented, and uneven across countries.

1. Why Carbon Markets Matter for East Africa

According to the World Bank, carbon markets could channel over $100 billion annually to developing countries by 2030. Africa, with its natural assets and low emissions profile, could supply up to 30% of the world’s carbon credits by 2030 under favorable policy and institutional conditions (McKinsey, 2022).

The East Africa region is home to some of the world’s most significant carbon sinks, including: The Congo Basin peatlands in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), storing ~29 billion tonnes of carbon (UNEP, 2021), The Albertine Rift forests and wetlands in Uganda and Kenya’s extensive mangrove and savannah ecosystems. Moreover, East Africa holds some of the world’s highest renewable energy potential, with Africa accounting for 60% of the best solar resources globally but possessing only 1% of installed solar PV capacity (IEA, 2022). In the Greater Horn of Africa, hydropower contributes around two-thirds of electricity generation, while geothermal accounts for nearly one-fifth (IEA, 2024). Additionally, smallholder farmers—who manage approximately 80% of sub-Saharan Africa’s farmland and contribute up to 70% of the continent’s food supply (Farm Africa, 2024; IFAD, 2024)—represent a critical force for scaling climate-smart agriculture and sustainable land use across the region.

2. Current Market Landscape in East Africa

The market data shows a dynamic yet uneven carbon market landscape across East Africa. Countries like Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda have made significant strides in scaling project pipelines and aligning with international mechanisms, while others like Burundi and South Sudan are in earlier stages of engagement.

Kenya, continues to lead the region, with nearly 300 registered carbon projects. These span geothermal and wind energy, reforestation, and clean cooking initiatives. Kenya is also the first in the region to publish regulations aligned with Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, positioning it as a policy frontrunner.

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement allows countries to cooperate in meeting their climate goals, mainly through carbon markets: Article 6.2, Lets countries trade carbon credits (called ITMOs) to meet their targets; Article 6.4, Sets up a UN-supervised global carbon market for public and private actors; and Article 6.8, Supports non-market approaches like cooperation and knowledge sharing. Article 6 helps countries reduce emissions more cost-effectively while promoting international climate action.

Uganda, with approximately 250 active projects, is building momentum through forestry and energy access projects. The government’s engagement in pilot Article 6 programs reflects early-stage ambition. The 2021 Climate Change Act still requires operationalization for scale-up.

Rwanda, maintains over 200 active projects and is among the first in Africa to issue multiple Letters of Authorization (LoAs) for Article 6.2 transactions. It is also piloting a national digital MRV platform.

Tanzania, possesses strong natural capital but lags in regulatory preparedness. With only ~48 active projects, the country is finalizing its Article 6 framework. Private developers continue to innovate, especially in REDD+ and land use.

Burundi and South Sudan, remain nascent markets. Each has fewer than 5 known carbon projects, largely in early-stage clean energy or forestry. While there is international donor interest, formal Article 6 engagement is minimal.

Democratic Republic of Congo, stands out for the scale of its forest coverage. Although specific project numbers are elusive, more than 54 million hectares of land are under carbon-related activities. DRC is active in bilateral Article 6 dialogues but lacks formal market infrastructure.

3. Opportunities for Growth

A. Natural Resource Endowment

East Africa boasts over 130 million hectares of forest cover, according to the FAO, and major wetlands and savannahs with high carbon sequestration potential. The Congo Basin alone stores an estimated 29 billion tonnes of carbon in its peatlands (UNEP, 2021).

B. Energy Transition

East Africa is advancing its clean energy transition, with Kenya generating 92.3% of electricity from renewables and Uganda exceeding 1,000 MW in installed renewable capacity (EPRA, 2024; ERA, 2024). These developments support energy-related carbon credit opportunities.

Beyond grid power, a major untapped market lies in shifting households away from biomass fuels like firewood and charcoal. Over 900 million people in sub-Saharan Africa still lack access to clean cooking (WLPGA, 2024), and meeting this need requires $8 billion annually in investment (OPEC Fund, 2024).

Solutions like improved cookstoves, biogas, LPG, and electric cooking are expanding across the region, many supported by carbon finance.

BURN Manufacturing’s clean cookstoves are projected to avoid 12 million tonnes of CO₂ and reach 6.5 million people, funded largely through carbon credit revenues (EIB, 2024).

Scaling these alternatives offers a powerful carbon market opportunity—combining emissions reductions with health, gender, and development benefits.

C. Climate-Smart Agriculture

Agriculture is a cornerstone of East African economies, contributing approximately 30–40% of GDP within the East African Community (EAC). Implementing sustainable practices such as agroforestry and soil carbon enhancement not only bolsters climate resilience but also offers significant opportunities for carbon sequestration. Countries like Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda have initiated projects utilizing methodologies like Verra's VM0042 Improved Agricultural Land Management, aiming to quantify and monetize these environmental benefits.

Project Examples:

1) Kenya Agricultural Carbon Project (KACP): Launched in 2014, KACP became the first initiative to earn carbon credits under the Verified Carbon Standard (VCS) for soil carbon sequestration. Engaging 60,000 farmers across 45,000 hectares, the project achieved a reduction of 24,788 metric tons of CO₂ in its initial issuance;

2) Boomitra Carbon Farming in East Africa: This project spans Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, and Uganda, focusing on improved cropland management. With a projected annual removal of approximately 300,000 tCO₂e, the initiative is set for a 20-year crediting period starting from October 2019.

D. Youth and Innovation

The region is home to over 100 climate-tech startups (AfriLabs, 2024), especially in Kenya and Rwanda, exploring blockchain-based MRV, satellite monitoring, and digital platforms for credit traceability and smallholder farmer inclusion.

4. Challenges to Market Maturity

Despite its promise, East Africa’s carbon market landscape faces several challenges:

A. Regulatory Uncertainty

Only a few EAC countries have passed climate change legislation that explicitly governs carbon markets. While most countries still lack national registries or Article 6 readiness frameworks.

  • Kenya passed the Climate Change Act (2016); implementing a national carbon registry

  • Rwanda has passed Law N°48/2018 on the Environment; implementing a national carbon registry

  • Uganda has passed the National Climate Change Act (2021); implementing a national carbon registry

  • Tanzania operates under the Environment Management. Act No. 20 of 2004 but recently passed the Environmental Management (Control and Management of Carbon Trading) Regulations, 2022; have a national carbon registry with a pipeline of 75+ projects

  • DRC operates under the Environment Protection Law 2011, but no dedicated climate change law; no national carbon registry

  • Burundi, no dedicated climate change legislation, completed a REDD+National Strategy and Action Plan; no national carbon registry

  • South Sudan operates under the The Environmental Protection Act, 2001, but no dedicated climate change policies or law, completed a National Adaptation Plan (NAP) for Climate Change in 2021; no national carbon registry

  • DRC operates under the Environment Protection Law 2011, but no dedicated climate change law; no national carbon registry

B. Fragmented and Costly Verification

High transaction and verification costs (exceed $100,000+ per project), with long lead times (12–24 months). This creates barriers for community-led and small-scale projects and make carbon markets inaccessible. This limits local ownership and equity. A possible solution is to establish a regional carbon project aggregation and digitized MRV platform that reduces costs and improves access for small-scale and community-led projects.

Case Study:

Aggregation Model – Ghana Improved Cookstove PoA: Ghana’s Improved Cookstove Programme of Activities (PoA) shows how aggregation can reduce costs and open up carbon markets for small-scale projects. By bundling multiple clean cooking initiatives under a single carbon framework, the program cut transaction costs by up to 70% and enabled local stove producers and NGOs to access carbon finance. Over 100,000 cookstoves were distributed, generating hundreds of thousands of carbon credits, with shared MRV processes and streamlined verification cycles reducing lead times and costs. This model offers a practical blueprint for East Africa to scale community-led carbon projects efficiently and equitably.

C. Limited Market Infrastructure

East Africa currently lacks a regional carbon exchange or aggregator platform, making it difficult to discover fair prices for carbon credits or to bundle small-scale projects into investable portfolios. Without this infrastructure, countries often rely on third-party international project developers to access carbon markets. While these actors bring technical expertise, their involvement often leads to value leakage, where a significant portion of the carbon revenue leaves local communities and countries.

A potential solution is to establish a regional carbon exchange or aggregator platform that enables price transparency, project bundling, and direct access to buyers. Such a platform could help small, community-led projects overcome high transaction costs by pooling resources and standardizing verification processes. It would also improve local ownership and ensure a greater share of the carbon finance stays within East Africa. Governments, private actors, and development partners could co-invest in building this infrastructure as part of a broader effort to scale high-integrity carbon markets in the region.

D. Integrity and Greenwashing Concerns

As global demand for carbon credits grows, so does scrutiny—particularly on projects in Africa—over risks of greenwashing and weak environmental claims. Buyers increasingly seek high-integrity credits that demonstrate real, additional, and lasting emissions reductions, along with strong social and environmental safeguards.

To stay competitive, East African projects must align with emerging global standards such as the Core Carbon Principles (CCPs) from the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market (ICVCM) and and the Claims Code of Practice from the Voluntary Carbon Markets Integrity Initiative (VCMI) frameworks to stay competitive. These frameworks promote transparency, rigorous verification, and credible use of carbon credits. Adopting them will help East African countries build trust, attract more investment, and ensure that carbon finance delivers genuine climate and community benefits.

E. Capacity and Awareness Gaps

Many national agencies and local developers in East Africa lack the technical skills needed to design and finance carbon projects, especially in areas like MRV and structuring for carbon markets. A 2021 review by the Eastern Africa Alliance on Carbon Markets found that most countries still rely on outdated CDM-era systems and need significant upgrades to participate effectively in Article 6 (Perspectives Climate Group, 2023). The African Carbon Markets Initiative also highlights the urgent need to build local capacity and scale up African project developers (PMI Climate, 2023). Without targeted support, local actors risk being sidelined, and much of the carbon market value could be lost to external intermediaries.

5. The Way Forward

To unlock carbon market potential, EAC countries must take coordinated action:

National-Level Actions

  • Finalize and implement national carbon market frameworks aligned with updated NDCs and Article 6.2/6.4 mechanisms of the Paris Agreement.

  • Establish or enhance domestic carbon registries with digital traceability tools to improve transparency and avoid double counting.

  • Develop benefit-sharing frameworks to ensure fair and inclusive distribution of carbon revenues, especially at the community level.

  • Integrate ICVCM and VCMI standards into national systems to build trust and meet the growing demand for high-integrity credits.

Regional Collaboration

  • Create a regional carbon exchange or aggregator platform (e.g. East African Carbon Exchange – EACEX) to support price discovery, pooled project access, and matchmaking between buyers and sellers.

  • Harmonize MRV protocols and integrity standards across EAC countries to reduce duplication, lower verification costs, and boost buyer confidence.

  • Coordinate Article 6 engagement through shared infrastructure and joint negotiations to strengthen regional leverage in international markets.

Capacity Building

  • Invest in hands-on training for public agencies, developers, and communities on carbon methodologies, MRV systems, and project finance structuring.

  • Support local climate tech startups and service providers through innovation hubs, accelerators, and blended finance vehicles.

  • Provide targeted technical assistance for community-led and nature-based projects, helping them meet verification and registration requirements.

  • Build long-term institutional capacity through partnerships with universities, vocational institutions, and regional climate research centers.

Conclusion

Carbon markets offer East Africa a chance to access new financing streams, drive climate-smart innovation, and position itself as a global supplier of high-quality carbon credits. But to do so, the region must overcome governance, institutional, and technical barriers—and invest in readiness now.

A coordinated, high-integrity approach will be essential to ensure that carbon finance delivers real emissions reductions, supports sustainable development, and benefits local communities equitably.
