The World Is Reordering. Africa Must Be the Centre

Originally published on Substack: 2025-04-10 · Original publication ↗

Ray Dalio’s Changing World Order reminds us that no global power stays on top forever. In his analysis, the rise and fall of empires follows a familiar rhythm: economic strength fuels influence and military power, but debt, inequality, and internal division eventually erode that dominance.

See chart below.

As the United States faces political fragmentation, and China rises as a challenger, the world is moving into a new, multipolar reality. For Africa, this presents a rare window—not just to adjust to change, but to shape it.

Africa should no longer be a passive observer in global affairs. With a population set to reach 2.5 billion by 2050—over 60% of whom will be under 25—Africa holds the last great demographic advantage. It is home to critical mineral resources, the world’s highest solar potential, and vibrant digital ecosystems that have leapfrogged traditional infrastructure. Yet its role in the next world order will depend not on what it has, but on what it does with it.

To claim a central role, Africa must build a capable political economy and invest in state capacity. That includes long-term policymaking, institutional stability, infrastructure planning, and capital market development. Some countries are already showing the way: Rwanda’s state-led innovation, Kenya’s fintech dynamism, and Ghana’s growing fiscal maturity are examples of what intentional governance can achieve. But elsewhere, corruption, volatility, and over dependence on donor financing still dominate, for example see effects of USAID wothdrawal.

So what, where is Africa's action plan?

The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) provides a once-in-a-generation platform for integration. It links 54 countries in a $3.4 trillion market. However, policy without infrastructure is paper. Trade needs roads, rail, ports, and digital rails to flow. That’s where innovations like the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS) become vital.

PAPSS allows real-time cross-border payments in local currencies, bypassing the need for USD and reducing friction. It strengthens Africa’s financial sovereignty and accelerates intra-African trade. But PAPSS is just the plumbing. The continent must now build the architecture: logistics corridors, energy networks, data infrastructure, and industrial capacity.

This African economic transformation cannot be achieved without the infrastructure as the foundation, currently, only 16% of African trade is intra-continental. This is largely due to fragmented transport and poor connectivity. Also, industrialisation which is key for this economic transformation remains constrained by unreliable power—only 43% of Sub-Saharan Africa has access to grid electricity. Even thriving digital economies still rely on physical systems: fibre optic networks, cold storage, reliable logistics, and secure hubs.

Africa’s renewable energy potential presents a solution to the unreliable power problem. While the solar potential in Africa is acknowledged, there is potential that goes far beyond solar. Untapped hydropower, powerful wind corridors, geothermal resources in the Rift Valley, and growing interest in green hydrogen suggest Africa could one day become a clean energy exporter. But these opportunities must be captured through intentional and directed investment, not left to chance.

Africa therefore needs to move up the value chain—from resource extraction to green manufacturing and innovation. That means refining minerals, assembling batteries, and leading in climate-smart technology. This won’t happen without capital—and lots of it.

Africa’s own capital base—over $200 billion in pensions, sovereign wealth, and insurance—is underused. Mobilising this capital into infrastructure, private credit, and industrial ventures is necessary. But it’s not enough. To truly reposition Africa in the global hierarchy, the continent needs an estimated $1.2–$1.5 trillion over the next decade.

Blended finance is the most strategic tool to bridge this investment gap. By combining concessional, philanthropic, and commercial capital, Africa can de-risk high-impact sectors and attract long-term private investment. Vehicles like Africa50 and Room2Run prove that structured capital can unlock 4–6x the value in private flows.

Africa must stop asking for inclusion—and start asserting its centrality. It has the numbers. It has the natural resources. It has the youth, the markets, and increasingly, the digital architecture. But the next global centre won’t be inherited. It must be built—through infrastructure, financial innovation, governance reform, and visionary investment.

The world is reordering. For the first time in modern history, Africa is not just being shaped by this change—it has a chance to shape it.
