Three Models of Institutional Continuity: Cooperative, Bank and Airline

Bugisu Co-operative Union, Banque Misr and Ethiopian Airlines pose three different questions about how organisations endure.

Kenneth S. Legesi · 19 October 2026 · Original editorial feature

Why comparison matters

The search for Africa's oldest enterprises is most useful when it becomes a comparison of institutional design. A cooperative, a bank and a national airline operate under different incentives, legal forms and capital requirements. The purpose of comparing them is not to crown a universal model but to identify what each arrangement must do to remain viable after its founders or first managers depart.

Bugisu: continuity through member institutions

Bugisu Co-operative Union's official history places its registration in July 1954, after the consolidation of four local unions. An earlier Bugisu Coffee Board dates to 1933, but that predecessor should not be treated as the union's own incorporation date. The cooperative model raises questions about how primary societies elect leadership, discipline management, accumulate capital and represent members whose interests evolve. Original registration and audited financial records remain necessary to establish full continuity.

Banque Misr: continuity through changes of control

Banque Misr traces its establishment to 1920 under Mohamed Talaat Harb. The bank's own historical publications report the founder's resignation in 1939 and nationalisation in 1960. The analytical issue is what survived these transitions: a legal entity, banking franchise, deposit relationships, managerial routines, public mandate or all of them. A founding date alone cannot answer that question; historical shareholder and statutory records are needed.

Ethiopian Airlines: continuity through organisational capability

Ethiopian Airlines reports founding on 21 December 1945 and commencement of operations on 8 April 1946, with government ownership. In 2022, the airline announced the appointment of Mesfin Tasew as group CEO after a long internal career. This makes the depth of the management pipeline an attractive hypothesis, but it does not prove that internal succession caused performance. A serious test would compare strategic continuity, governance decisions and financial results across leadership transitions.

The institutional common denominator

All three cases highlight the need to separate continuity of purpose from continuity of people. Member ownership can fail without accountable governance. State ownership can fail without commercial discipline. Professional management can fail without reliable oversight and capital allocation. The more promising research question is how each institution makes decisions, replenishes leadership, mobilises capital and corrects mistakes over time.

Evidence before rankings

The Africa's Enduring Enterprises directory is a provisional research register, not a definitive ranking. We publish uncertainty where records are incomplete and distinguish original corporate claims from independently corroborated legal and financial evidence. The aim is to learn how enduring African institutions are built, not to manufacture a league table from incomparable dates.
