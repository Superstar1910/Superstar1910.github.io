Unlocking Climate Finance for East Africa

Originally published on Substack: 2025-05-28 · Original publication ↗

🔁 This article is part of a 10-part series on Carbon Markets in East Africa - a deep dive into climate finance, opportunity, and action across the region. To read the introduction to the series and the full article lineup, see here. In Article 1 I explored how carbon markets are rising across the region. This article looks at the broader climate finance ecosystem and the urgent need to move from ambition to execution.

The Scale and Urgency of the Climate Finance Gap

In Article 1, we saw how East Africa is being positioned as a major supplier of high-quality carbon credits. But the bigger question remains:

How will East Africa finance the full range of climate actions it has committed to under the Paris Agreement?

The cost of implementing Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) across the East African Community (EAC) is massive, and heavily reliant on external support.

While each country has made commitments to reduce emissions and enhance resilience, most lack the domestic fiscal space to finance their NDCs independently. In nearly all cases, more than 70% of NDC financing needs are conditional on international support through grants, concessional loans, and climate market instruments.

Source: UNFCCC NDC Registry, Country Submissions (2023)

Yet Africa receives just ~3% of global climate finance flows, and less than a third goes to adaptation, which is critical for East Africa’s resilience.

This underscores the urgent need to unlock climate finance flows into East Africa through coordinated strategies, catalytic mechanisms, and institutional readiness.

The Levers to Unlock Finance at Scale

To build on the foundation explored in Article 1 (carbon markets), EAC countries need to leverage three catalytic mechanisms:

1. Carbon Finance as a Catalyst, Not a Panacea

As explored in Part 1, carbon markets can do more than reduce emissions. They can unlock new sources of capital for clean energy, forestry, agriculture, and resilient infrastructure.

But their catalytic power depends on how well they are integrated into national finance strategies and development plans.

Without alignment with NDCs, community priorities, and credible verification, carbon markets risk becoming disconnected from local impact.

Despite rising interest, East Africa remains underrepresented in the global carbon credit market.

As shown above:

  • Kenya leads the region with 35 Verra-registered projects

  • Uganda (23), Tanzania (11), and Rwanda (8) follow

  • Burundi, DRC, and South Sudan remain largely absent

  • The entire EAC accounts for a small fraction of the over 500 Verra-registered projects in Africa

This gap reflects the absence of enabling infrastructure: registries, MRV systems, accredited entities, and investor-ready pipelines.

To fully leverage carbon markets, EAC countries must go beyond project-by-project registration. They need:

  • National registries aligned with Article 6 of the Paris Agreement

  • Clear rules on benefit-sharing and community safeguards

  • Investment platforms that link verified carbon assets to global buyers

Carbon finance can be a gamechanger - but only when built into a coordinated climate finance ecosystem.

2. Blended Finance: De-Risking the Pipeline

Private investors are often hesitant to finance climate projects in East Africa due to high perceived risks - from political uncertainty and currency volatility to limited project track records.

Blended finance addresses this challenge by strategically combining concessional capital (from donors, climate funds, or development banks) with commercial capital (from private investors). The concessional portion helps absorb early-stage risk or offer guarantees, making projects more attractive and viable for private participation.

Examples from the region:

Uganda’s Building Resilient Communities, Wetland Ecosystems and Associated Catchments project is an example of blended climate finance in action.

  • Lead Funder: Green Climate Fund – $24.1M grant

  • Co-funders: Government of Uganda (in-kind), UNDP (implementer)

  • Target: Restore 60,000 ha of wetlands in 24 districts (including Limoto Wetland, Pallisa District, Eastern Uganda)

  • Beneficiaries: ~800,000 people

  • Focus: Nature-based adaptation, climate-resilient livelihoods, and local capacity building

The project uses grant capital to de-risk restoration and community livelihood activities (e.g., fish farming, eco-tourism, rice irrigation), while strengthening institutional climate planning. A model for EAC countries leveraging concessional capital to unlock ecosystem-based adaptation and rural resilience.

The Global Energy Alliance for People and Planet (GEAPP) is supporting the rollout of solar mini-grids across Nigeria, rural Kenya and Rwanda through a blended finance model designed to accelerate clean energy access.

  • Funders: GEAPP, Rockefeller Foundation, IKEA Foundation, Bezos Earth Fund

  • Model: Concessional capital blended with private investment

  • Focus: Off-grid solar systems for rural households, SMEs, and public facilities

  • Technology: Solar PV + batteries + smart meters

  • Implementation Period: 2021–2025

  • Notable Investment: $10M GEAPP anchor investment in Nigeria’s Energy Transition & Access Facility for Africa (ETAFA) facility catalyzed $50M total - a model extendable to East Africa

A scalable model combining donor de-risking, private sector delivery, and public sector support. Mini-grids are powering last-mile communities and proving investable with the right capital stack.

3. National Climate Finance Institutions: The Anchor Missing in Most EAC Countries

While carbon markets and blended finance can catalyze climate action, they depend on strong national institutions to access, manage, and scale climate finance.

Across the EAC, this is the least developed link in the climate finance chain.

Of all EAC countries, only Rwanda has established a robust national climate finance institution - FONERWA, the Rwanda Green Fund.

Why National Climate Finance Institutions Matter

These institutions serve as:

  • Access points to international climate funds like the Green Climate Fund (GCF), Adaptation Fund (AF), and Climate Investment Funds (CIF)

  • Coordinators across ministries, local governments, and development partners

  • Deployers of capital to vetted, bankable, and inclusive projects

  • Trackers of climate finance impact and reporting obligations under the Paris Agreement

Without them, countries rely on external intermediaries, face disjointed project flows, and struggle to link climate finance with national development planning.

Case Study: FONERWA

FONERWA (Rwanda Green Fund) is widely recognized as the most effective national climate finance platform in the East African region.

  • Strong governance and fiduciary systems

  • Robust project pipeline development, aligned with national priorities

  • Trusted by donors and multilaterals as a direct access entity

Results:

  • Over $250 million mobilized from partners like GCF, UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO), and African Development Bank (AfDB)

  • More than 40 projects funded, spanning adaptation, mitigation, and resilience

FONERWA offers a replicable model for other EAC countries seeking to scale climate finance through domestic institutions.

What EAC Countries Should Do

To strengthen climate finance readiness, governments should:

  • Establish dedicated national climate finance institutions (either new or embedded within existing development banks)

  • Pursue GCF accreditation or partner with accredited entities on a pathway to independence

  • Develop a national climate investment plan to align projects with funding windows

  • Build capacity for MRV, fiduciary standards, and project selection

Without institutional readiness, even the best carbon market or finance mechanism remains untapped potential.

From Readiness to Results

Individually, carbon markets, blended finance, and national climate institutions offer powerful tools. But without a supporting ecosystem to activate them, these mechanisms risk remaining underutilized.

The current reality is clear, most East African countries are not yet ready to fully absorb or scale climate finance. Despite updated NDCs and growing interest from global investors, the enabling systems are still underdeveloped.

Common barriers include:

  • Lack of national climate finance strategies to prioritize and coordinate action

  • Weak project pipelines, especially outside energy and forestry

  • Underdeveloped MRV systems and absence of digital carbon registries

  • Limited institutional capacity to design, fund, and monitor projects

Summary by country

  • Rwanda is the clear regional leader, with a dedicated climate finance institution (FONERWA), strong MRV systems, a maturing carbon registry, and a structured project pipeline.

  • Kenya has a published strategy and functioning MRV systems. It is piloting a national registry and has an active project pipeline - especially in energy and agriculture.

  • Uganda is in a transitional phase. It has engaged with the Green Climate Fund and developed project pilots (e.g. wetlands restoration), but lacks a formal finance strategy or registry.

  • Tanzania, Burundi, South Sudan, and DRC are still in early stages. These countries have yet to establish foundational systems such as national climate strategies, MRV frameworks, or formal project pipelines.

Only Kenya and Rwanda have made meaningful progress toward linking their NDCs with national financing strategies and investment pipelines. Others remain stuck at the planning stage.

What is needed now is not just more funding but a deliberate shift from fragmented efforts to a structured, country-owned climate finance architecture.

Steps to Accelerate Results

  1. Integrate Climate Finance into National Development Planning
    Embed climate finance priorities into national budgets, economic blueprints, and sector investment strategies - not just environmental policy.

  2. Establish or Strengthen National Climate Finance Institutions
    Set up dedicated funds or empower development banks to act as central nodes for climate finance aggregation, deployment, and coordination.

  3. Operationalize Carbon Market Readiness
    Build national registries, set up Article 6 policy frameworks, and align carbon project pipelines with NDC targets and benefit-sharing rules.

  4. Use Blended Finance as a System, Not an Exception
    Scale concessional instruments to unlock private investment in key sectors: energy access, water security, nature-based solutions, and resilient agriculture.

  5. Invest in Capacity and Data Infrastructure
    Develop MRV systems, build local expertise in climate finance structuring, and digitize project tracking and reporting tools.

Quick Reference: Bridging the Climate Finance Gap

To translate climate ambition into investable action, EAC countries should:

  • Enact national carbon market frameworks (registries, Article 6 rules, benefit-sharing policies)

  • Develop coordinated project pipelines and matchmaking platforms

  • Invest in MRV systems and digital traceability tools

  • Build capacity across Ministries of Finance, development banks, and project originators

  • Engage the African Carbon Markets Initiative (ACMI) and participate in early Article 6 transactions

The transition from climate ambition to investable action requires not just more capital, but better systems.

Conclusion

East Africa has the ambition and the raw potential. What it now needs is speed, systems, and structure.

Carbon markets may be the ignition, but the engine of transformation is a regional climate finance ecosystem built on institutions, transparency and bankable projects.
