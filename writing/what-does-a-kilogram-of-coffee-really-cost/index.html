RESEARCH / COFFEE PRICING ENGINE

Kenneth S. Legesi, CFA · 29 October 2026 · Research essay

What Does a Kilogram of Coffee Really Cost?

The denominator matters as much as the numerator.

Coffee businesses often discuss cost per kilogram without first agreeing on which kilogram they mean.

Is it freshly harvested cherry? Dried cherry (Kiboko)? Parchment? Hulled FAQ? Clean green beans? A particular screen grade accepted by an export buyer? These are not interchangeable products. Nor do they have the same weight, moisture, processing losses or price.

Before we calculate the true cost of coffee, we must first reconcile its physical journey.

Begin with the productive tree

Consider a purely illustrative coffee plot with 450 productive trees and 600 kilograms of saleable green-coffee-equivalent output per acre. That is approximately 1.33 kilograms of green equivalent per productive tree. This is not a claimed Uganda yield standard; it simply shows how a farm-level assumption becomes a unit-cost denominator.

If attributable farm costs are UGX 6 million per year and the plot produces 600 saleable green-equivalent kilograms, the arithmetic cost is UGX 10,000 per kilogram. With the same costs but 400 kilograms, unit cost rises to UGX 15,000.

The exercise is simple; doing it correctly is not. Establishment costs, tree age, unpaid family labour, depreciation, land opportunity cost and planting losses must be classified consistently. An annual cash break-even measure and a full economic cost are both useful, but answer different questions.

One input kilogram does not become one export kilogram

The next challenge is physical conversion. Coffee loses weight as moisture is removed, hulls are separated, defects rejected and beans sorted into saleable grades.

Suppose a hypothetical mill buys 1,000 kilograms of a dry input form, recovers 550 kilograms of green output, and only 495 kilograms meet a specified target grade. The remaining output may still have saleable value. Charging all input costs against 495 kilograms without accounting for other saleable grades can distort profitability; dividing the purchase price by 1,000 kilograms understates the cost of target-grade output.

A proper costing system needs an input-output mass balance, a recorded moisture basis, grade-by-grade recovery and an explicit method for allocating joint costs or crediting by-product revenue. No blanket conversion factor can substitute for an actual mill-batch reconciliation.

From production cost to commercial quote

The farmer's sale price becomes the trader's purchase cost. The trader adds sourcing, handling, transport, processing, storage, cost of money and contract performance risk. The exporter must consider grade, named delivery place, buyer payment, logistics and currency. A farmer's sale in Kampala is not the same economic proposition as delivery under an ocean-freight contract.

The fully loaded cost is one number. The relevant incremental cost of accepting a particular buyer offer can be another. The market price might be above or below either.

That is why the proposed Coffee Pricing Engine separates the cost-based minimum and target quote from a comparable, dated buyer price. A software-generated number should not be confused with an executable trade.

The research question

We are developing a Uganda-first unit-cost framework that starts at the tree and follows the coffee through farm, mill, lot, grade and sale. The aim is to expose assumptions, test them against records and explain where price and cost diverge.

A useful pricing engine should make its assumptions contestable — not hide them.
