What you should know before you participate in an Initial Coin Offering (ICO)

Original publication ↗

After a warning from Uganda’s central bank about One Coin, https://www.bou.or.ug/bou/media/statements/One-Coin-Digital-Money-operations-in-Uganda.html there continue to be anecdotal stories about other coins claimed to be Ponzi schemes. Inspite of this, there is still some interest in cryptocurrencies which are traded in the secondary market and associated Initial Coin Offerings (ICOs) as an investment opportunity. Just so I am clear, this does not constitute investment advice. I just thought it pertinent to share some general information regarding Cryptocurrencies and ICOs as a buyer be ware for those seeking to participate in this exciting but very highly speculative and risky area.

What are ICOs? ICOs are the process by which cryptocurrencies are created, launched to the market before they trade in a secondary market. Prior to listing in the secondary market, a cryptocurrency/coin comes into form through an Initial Coin Offering (ICO). An ICO can be thought of or paralleled to an Initial Public Offering (IPO) for trading a stock for the first time, subsequently the coin or stock trades in the secondary market.

However, even before one participates in an ICO, just like participating in any venture, it is important to understand the why, what and wherefores of a particular investment. I share below some of the things to look out for before diving into owning a cryptocurrency or participating in an ICO.

This has been gained from reading up on the subject and discussions with others I consider more knowledgeable than me on the subject.

Where to start? A good resource for viewing all coins trading in the secondary market is CoinMarketCap, https://coinmarketcap.com/all/views/all/. This lists all the coins trading post ICO in a secondary market. If it does not exist on Coin Market Cap, it probably is not a legitimate coin. As of 26/09/2018, there were 2003 coins listed at Coin Market Cap. CoinMarketCap also lists the exchanges where coins are traded. To buy into or sell a coin, one would have to do so through one of the exchanges listed on CoinMarketCap.

The top five (5) exchanges by volume traded as listed on CoinMarketCap in order are Binance, OKEx, Bitfinex, Huobi and Bithumb. However, it is also possible to trade coins through willing buyer and seller off the exchange straight from wallets where the coins are held. Examples of wallets include Blockchain Wallet, Binance Wallet.

What should you look out for before you participate in an ICO, how do you identify a genuine ICO? Most of the information below will be available at the ICO project site:

  1. Coin and use case: What is the proposed coin and the associated use case, does this make sense, is there an economic rationale? What is the history of the founders, what problem are they trying to solve with block chain, does it fit with a relatable use case?
  2. Block chain and protocol: What is the blockchain being used and the associated protocol? This will give you an understanding of the block chain properties, will scale of the block chain be easy, will demand be attracted to the block chain, how quickly are transactions processed on the blockchain, is the associated protocol secure and relatively hack proof? How does the protocol and blockchain model compare to peers?
  3. White paper: Is there a white paper. A white paper is a document which includes an outline of a problem that the block chain project is looking to solve, the solution to that problem as well as a detailed description of their product, its architecture and its interaction with users.. For example a white paper database exists here https://whitepaperdatabase.com/
  4. Community Support: Is there a community associated with the coin or is one proposed, what activities does the community support for example it is common for a community to issue out grants to people/startups that are building products around the blockchain associated with a given cryptocurrency.
  5. Supply: How will supply be controlled, how much of the coin is freely traded, how will it be mined. If supply is very limited and there is a string use case, it may be expected that the coin will increase in value as demand soars

After you have considered all the issues outlined above and you still want to proceed with participation in an ICO, there is some guidance here https://cointelegraph.com/ico-101/how-to-buy-ico-tokens-beginners-guide#how-to-buy-tokens-during-an-ico, to summarise the steps:

  1. Register for an ICO through the project’s website: Beware of ICO campaigns that don’t require any registration whatsoever.
  2. Get Bitcoin or Ether: the major and stable cryptocurrencies that will most likely be used for participating in the ICO
  3. Move your Bitcoins or Ether to a wallet you control: this maybe a software wallet or hardware wallet.
  4. Buy ICO tokens: Once registered for an ICO and have funds available and ready, to buy ICO tokens, send your cryptocurrency to the campaign’s address.
  5. Participate in an ICO by sending your crypto to the project’s address: check the website’s address as many times as is necessary. There might be fake ICO websites listed as ads on top of your Google search results. Those will look identical to actual websites, with one or two symbols of the address being slightly different.
  6. You get ICO tokens to your address: You should receive your newly purchased tokens to your wallet’s address.

Good luck investing! See you at the next ICO.
