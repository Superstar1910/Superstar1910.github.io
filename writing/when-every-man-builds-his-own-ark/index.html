When every man builds his own ark, civilization drowns

Originally published on Substack: 2025-11-03 · Original publication ↗

Disclaimer: This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute investment advice. Please consult your financial advisor before making any investment decisions.

Last week, a post by Ian Gumisiriza, better known as @T_E_F_L_O_N, stirred heated debate on X (formerly Twitter).
He wrote:

“VERY UNPOPULAR OPINION: Kampala was flooding long before Ham started any works on Nakivubo Channel.”

The post did what storms of rain often do to Kampala: it exposed what lay beneath the surface.

Replies poured in.
Some blamed the construction works for blocking drainage and worsening the floods.
Others argued the floods had been inevitable - that the real culprits were decades of poor planning, encroached wetlands and absent accountability.
But hidden beneath the noise was a deeper truth: Kampala was not flooded by rain alone. It was flooded by rational decisions made in a broken system.

The city didn’t flood because of Ham. It flooded because the system that enabled him behaves exactly as designed.

This article explores the idea, The tragedy of the commons, seen through the lens of a city drowning in its own logic. Every trader, builder and official acts rationally. Yet together, their actions create the perfect storm.

What Are We Really Seeing?

Before we go further, it helps to remember where the idea of the “Tragedy of the Commons” came from.

The term was coined by the ecologist Garrett Hardin in a 1968 essay in Science.
He imagined a shared pasture, a commons, where each herder acts rationally by adding more cattle, because the private benefit goes entirely to them while the cost of overgrazing is shared by everyone. Over time, the pasture collapses under the weight of self-interest. The insight was not new. In 1833, economist William Forster Lloyd had described the same logic in his lectures on overgrazing in England’s communal lands. But Hardin’s version gave the metaphor its modern life.

When resources are shared but responsibility is not, rational behavior becomes destructive.

Today the commons are no longer fields; they are wetlands, drainage channels, city budgets, and public trust.

Kampala’s floods are simply a modern version of the same story, flooding is the surface symptom of something deeper, a social equilibrium where every participant acts rationally within a broken system.

The trader rents a cheaper stall in the valley because that’s what they can afford.

The developer builds there because land at higher ground costs more.

The official approves it because fees and favors are quicker than enforcement.

And the taxpayer buys a piece of land on higher ground wherever they can, fences off their house, buys a generator and hires private security because “that’s how the country works.”

Each decision makes sense in isolation. Together, they create the tragedy of the commons: individually rational behavior producing collective ruin.

The commons are a fragile network of trust. When that trust is monetized, privatized, or abandoned, the network collapses. What we call a “natural disaster” is often just the bill for years of deferred responsibility.

Where did the System break?

Our problem is not ignorance. It is misaligned incentives. The people in charge know the blueprints, the cost estimates, the hydrological data, the case studies and benchmarks - I too explored these themes in my post on X.

What the people in charge lack is the motivation to act when accountability carries no personal price.

Citizens, meanwhile, have stopped believing. They pay taxes but expect nothing back. Corruption, like erosion, starts small and becomes a landscape. Soon, distrust becomes policy: everyone protects themselves first, because nobody expects protection from the system.

“Corruption” here means a system that has worn down - where honesty, accountability and public duty fade away, until people are forced to look out for themselves instead of the community - see more under Notes and References at the end of this article.

Consequently, risk ends up sorting people by what they can afford.

Those with resources relocate uphill, insure their assets, or leave the country altogether. Those without resources rebuild in the same place, hoping next time the water will be kinder. The same flood, two realities.

In Kampala, this stratification is visible from the air: wealth on the ridges, vulnerability in the valleys. When resilience becomes a private luxury, the commons dies quietly.

How do we escaping this Collective Trap?

The temptation is to demand better government. But governance is only as strong as the culture that sustains it. No policy can substitute for a people who have abdicated their shared responsibility. To rebuild the commons, we must invert the model: from dependence to participation, from hierarchy to agency.

1. Radical Transparency as Default

Corruption survives in shadows. Transparency is not a website or a dashboard; it’s a social norm. Citizens must expect to see what is collected, what is spent, and what was promised. When people can trace cause to effect - tax to drain, levy to culvert - the invisible hand of accountability reappears.

2. Local Stewardship Over Distant Authority

Centralized control breeds distance and apathy. But when management shifts to the smallest effective unit for example the neighborhood, the parish, the street committee - people act because they see the consequences.

Collective maintenance, not municipal miracles, keeps drains clear and waste out. Responsibility must be as local as the water itself.

3. Shared Data, Shared Decisions

The next civic revolution is informational. When rainfall, blockage, and flood alerts are open to all, citizens coordinate faster than bureaucracy. Knowledge, once distributed, becomes self-correcting. If everyone sees the same truth, manipulation loses its edge.

4. Adaptive Insurance - Not Financial, but Social

Insurance is the pooling of risk among people who trust each other. It need not be sold by companies; it can be built through community savings groups, rotating funds, and local pacts. The principle is simple, those who stand together absorb shocks better than those who stand alone. When disaster hits, recovery should feel like reciprocity, not charity.

5. Skin in the Game at Every Level

The surest way to fix a system is to make the decision-makers live inside its consequences. Let the regulators who approve drainage projects commute through the same channels. Let the officials who sign off wetland developments reside where they license construction. Exposure breeds diligence; immunity breeds decay.

Without personal stakes, governance turns into performance art.

As Charlie Munger liked to remind us, “Show me the incentive and I’ll show you the outcome.” When leaders bear no cost for failure, the system quietly optimizes for appearances instead of results. Meetings multiply, reports thicken, and nothing real changes.

From Dependency to Decentralization

The flood is not merely water; it is information. It tells us where we have outsourced too much, trusted too little and acted too late.

A healthy society decentralizes by instinct, not by technology. It doesn’t wait for permission to cooperate. It builds redundant systems of care and accountability so that failure in one node doesn’t collapse the whole network - when the official drain fails, the community trench still works, when the contractor absconds, the neighborhood fixes what it can - such distributed responsibility is messy but antifragile. It learns faster than it breaks.

Dependence on central authority breeds fragility; distributed stewardship breeds resilience. Freedom, at its core, is the ability to correct mistakes locally before they become disasters nationally.

The Hill and the Valley

Every flood redraws the map of responsibility. It tells us who can escape and who must endure. Yet, even those on the hills are not safe; they merely postpone the reckoning. A city divided by altitude cannot survive indefinitely.

The water is impartial. It flows toward the path of least resistance, and right now, that path is moral, not physical. Until citizens rediscover the commons as an extension of themselves, the rain will keep teaching the same lesson.

In the end, resilience is not an engineering project. It is a collective decision to care, before the water arrives.

  3. Inspectorate of Government (IGG), Annual Integrity Surveys (2019–2023) — Cites corruption risks in land administration and urban planning, including issuance of wetland titles and informal payments for development permits.

  6. Ministry of Water & Environment, Wetlands Department (2022) - Reports approximately 70 % loss of Kampala’s wetlands since the 1990s, much of it involving officially sanctioned developments or titled land, indicating regulatory failure.

  7. Kampala Capital City Authority (KCCA) Budget Framework Papers 2015–2023: Drainage expenditure more than doubled over the period, while channel coverage and maintenance expansion lagged significantly, suggesting inefficiency and governance gaps.
