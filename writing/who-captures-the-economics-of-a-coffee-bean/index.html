Who Captures the Economics of a Coffee Bean?

Value is created and lost at every transfer from tree to buyer.

Coffee moves through a chain, but the economics do not move through it evenly. A farmer is managing biological productivity and seasonal liquidity; a processor is managing yield, moisture, labour and energy; a trader is financing time and inventory; an exporter is managing buyer specifications, freight, FX and settlement risk. To ask who captures value is first to ask which risks, costs and quantities each participant carries.

The kilogram is not constant through the chain. Fresh cherry, dried Kiboko, parchment and graded green coffee have different moisture levels and recoveries. If a trader purchases one tonne of dried coffee, the price paid on the input weight cannot be compared directly with the sale value of one tonne of grade-specific green output. Rejected beans and lower grades may still produce revenue, but only when recorded separately.

A price spread is not a margin. Suppose, purely as an illustration, a buyer quotes UGX 18,000/kg and the seller bought an input for UGX 12,000/kg. Without the purchased form, recovery rate, handling, finance and delivery point, the difference says very little about profit. Even the same trade can create different returns depending on when the seller pays suppliers and when the buyer pays. These figures are not observed market prices or verified quotations.

The Coffee Pricing Engine is an attempt to reveal those variables rather than hide them in a single price recommendation. The research begins at the productive tree, then follows weighed output, allocated costs, financing and a comparable buyer offer. The resulting data are not yet a representative map of Ugandan coffee profit margins; farm and trade records will have to establish that.

What the evidence tells us

Uganda's Ministry of Finance reported coffee exports of US$181.65 million in August 2026, down 10.4% from US$202.75 million a year earlier. The average unit value nevertheless increased from US$3.95 to US$4.10 per kilogram. Export volumes declined 13.76%. Higher prices per kilogram did not offset lower shipments. These are national averages across coffee varieties and grades, not Robusta farmgate prices or exporter profit margins. Source: MoFPED, September 2026 Performance of the Economy.

A 14 September 2026 report of MAAIF quotations put FAQ Robusta at UGX 11,500–12,500/kg at farmgate and Kiboko at UGX 5,000–5,800/kg. These are distinct physical forms; neither price is directly comparable to a kilogram of export-grade green coffee without verified out-turn and quality adjustments. Source: STN reporting MAAIF quotations.

One kilogram, many prices

Meaningful Vision reported an average £4.20 central London regular cappuccino price in June 2026, compared with £3.30 in June 2022. That is a retail beverage price, not a direct quote for Ugandan coffee. Source: Meaningful Vision, 25 September 2026.

Revenue is not profit

Comparing £180.60 of hypothetical café receipts with US$4.10/kg of Uganda's national average export unit value does not reveal a profit margin. The prices refer to different products, currencies, markets, dates and value-chain stages. The export average includes Arabica and Robusta; the café figure includes service and other inputs. A defensible distributional analysis requires matched quantities, grade, exchange rate, tax treatment, financing days and costs at each handover.

The 2024 Grounds for Sharing study offers a useful framework for separating costs, taxes and net profits in coffee chains. Its empirical country coverage should not be treated as a measurement of Uganda's margins.

What we still need to measure

The next research step is a matched Uganda-to-destination transaction dataset: dated farmgate purchase by physical form and grade; milling out-turn and by-product proceeds; exporter FOB invoice; freight and financing; importer/roaster costs; and café-level operating accounts. Only then can we estimate who captures net economic surplus, rather than confusing a price spread with profit.

Research invitation

We welcome anonymised examples of farm-year accounts, milling recovery sheets and completed coffee transactions, especially examples that contradict our assumptions. Contact us before sharing confidential records.
