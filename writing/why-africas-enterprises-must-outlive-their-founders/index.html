Why Africa’s Enterprises Must Outlive Their Founders

The question is not whether Africa can produce entrepreneurs. It is whether enterprise can become institution.

Kenneth S. Legesi · 19 October 2026 · Original editorial feature

A debate worth testing

A public conversation about whether an indigenous Ugandan business had survived for a century prompted a more important question: what does it take for a company to survive its founder? My response was simple: “Entrepreneurs start the businesses but never build the governance and institutions to ensure their longevity beyond the founder's vision.” The original exchange is here. The handwritten Uganda Business Longevity & Institution-Building Map developed that argument into a research agenda. A provocative social-media assertion is a hypothesis, not a census of historical enterprises.

The distinction between business and institution

A business can be commercially successful and still be organisationally fragile. Revenue, employment and even an impressive founder are insufficient tests of endurance. Institutions preserve decision rights, financial records, governance processes, managerial capability and access to capital when the individual who first assembled them is no longer in control. Longevity should therefore be measured not only in years but also through succession, ownership transfers, reinvestment and the capacity to survive shocks.

What the Uganda snapshot can and cannot tell us

The UBOS 2019/20 Census of Business Establishments provides a useful cross-sectional picture of enterprise structure. The handwritten map cites approximately 686,700 fixed-location establishments and a concentration of young, small and sole-proprietor businesses. These are starting points for checking the original census tables; they are not a longitudinal survival curve. In particular, the proportion of businesses currently older than fifty years cannot be interpreted as the probability that a newly established firm survives fifty years. Cohort data and business-register histories would be required for that claim.

Four different pathways

The cases in this series resist a single formula. Bugisu Co-operative Union raises questions about member governance and the continuity of a cooperative established in 1954. Banque Misr shows why founder departure and nationalisation must be analysed separately from institutional identity. Ethiopian Airlines invites scrutiny of long internal executive careers and state ownership. The Madhvani Group illustrates family-enterprise persistence through expropriation and the rehabilitation of industrial assets, even where operating-company continuity was broken. None of these examples establishes that a particular governance structure causes survival; each offers a testable proposition.

The agenda for institution-building

The practical agenda begins with separating founder and company assets, establishing reliable accounts and independent oversight, building a leadership bench, clarifying shareholder rights, and creating mechanisms for patient capital. It also requires legal enforcement, credible registries, functioning credit and equity markets, and the ability to reorganise distressed businesses without destroying productive capacity. The objective is not simply more start-ups. It is a larger stock of enterprises able to compound productive capital across generations.

How this series will work

Africa's Enduring Enterprises will maintain a public candidate directory, source register and documented case studies. We distinguish a founding story from legal incorporation, a family lineage from a company, and an announced financing package from actual disbursement. Corrections and new primary evidence are welcome. This first essay states the thesis; subsequent features examine the institutional mechanisms and the documentary record.
